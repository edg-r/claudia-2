## Ricardian Trade Model

The Ricardian model explains trade with **technology differences**. A country exports the good it can produce at the lowest opportunity cost, even if another country has absolute advantage in everything.

Know these cold:

- **Absolute advantage:** higher productivity in a good.
- **Comparative advantage:** lower opportunity cost in a good.
- **Marginal product of labor:** output per worker.
- **No-trade relative price:** the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other.
- **Gains from trade:** trade lets countries consume outside their no-trade production possibilities frontier.

Core formula:

```text
No-trade relative price of good X = opportunity cost of X
P_X / P_Y = MPL_Y / MPL_X
```

If one worker makes 2 cars or 3 TVs, the opportunity cost of 1 car is 3/2 TVs. The country with the lower relative price of cars has comparative advantage in cars.

Canvas trap to avoid:

- Do not pick the country with absolute advantage automatically.
- Do not compare raw labor productivity alone.
- Always convert to opportunity cost or no-trade relative price.

## Heckscher-Ohlin Model

The Heckscher-Ohlin model explains trade with **factor endowment differences**, not technology differences. A country exports the good that uses its abundant factor intensively.

Know these cold:

- **Factor abundance:** a country has relatively more of a factor, such as capital per worker, land per worker, or skilled labor share.
- **Factor intensity:** a good uses one factor relatively more heavily than another good.
- **Heckscher-Ohlin theorem:** countries export goods intensive in their abundant factors and import goods intensive in their scarce factors.
- **Trade triangle:** the difference between what a country produces and what it consumes under trade.
- **Leontief paradox:** U.S. trade data appeared to contradict the simple HO prediction that the U.S. should export capital-intensive goods.

Fast solution pattern:

1. Identify the country's abundant factor.
2. Identify the good's intensive factor.
3. Match abundance to intensity.
4. Predict exports and imports.
5. If asked about distribution, move to Stolper-Samuelson.

Example:

If Japan imports rice and rice is labor-intensive, Japan is labor-scarce and capital-abundant. Opening to trade lowers Japan's relative price of rice, so labor loses and capital gains.

## Stolper-Samuelson

Stolper-Samuelson is the distributional punchline of Heckscher-Ohlin. When the relative price of a good rises, the real return to the factor used intensively in that good rises, and the real return to the other factor falls.

Fast solution pattern:

1. Which good's relative price rose?
2. Which factor is used intensively in that good?
3. That factor gains in real terms.
4. The other factor loses in real terms.

If shoes become capital-intensive because factories automate and the relative price of shoes rises, capital owners gain and workers lose.

## Mini Self-Test

1. A country can produce 4 bikes or 6 snowboards per worker. What is the opportunity cost of one bike?
2. Home has a lower no-trade relative price of textiles than Foreign. What does Home export?
3. Canada is capital-abundant and robots are capital-intensive. What does HO predict Canada exports?
4. A country imports a labor-intensive good. Which domestic factor likely opposes free trade?
5. Why can a country gain from trade even when it has absolute advantage in both goods?

## Answer Key

1. 6/4 = 3/2 snowboards.
2. Textiles.
3. Robots.
4. Labor, because the scarce factor loses under Stolper-Samuelson.
5. Because trade follows comparative advantage, not absolute advantage. Specialization and exchange can put consumption on a higher indifference curve than autarky.
