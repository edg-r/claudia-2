## Core Question

How do weak actors change the behavior of stronger actors, and when can institutions make collective restraint possible? Kydd and Walter treat terrorism as strategic signaling under uncertainty. Stein and Gourevitch widen the lens: collective security depends on regimes and governance institutions that can coordinate or constrain actors, but those institutions are themselves political objects that actors may build, exploit, or contest.

## Reading Map

**Kydd & Walter:** terrorism is not random violence. It is a form of **costly signaling** used by nonstate actors who are too weak to impose outcomes directly. Their key move is to separate terrorist **goals** from terrorist **strategies**. The five major strategies are **attrition** (make the target believe continued policy is too costly), **intimidation** (make civilians believe the group can punish noncompliance and the state cannot protect them), **provocation** (goad the state into indiscriminate retaliation that radicalizes the population), **spoiling** (destroy trust between moderates and the target), and **outbidding** (use violence to prove greater resolve than rival groups). The policy implication is sharp: counterterrorism fails when the state responds to the wrong strategy.

**Stein:** international regimes exist when actors move from independent decision making to **joint decision making**. Regimes are not just organizations or any patterned behavior. They arise when self-interested actors face either **dilemmas of common interests** or **dilemmas of common aversions**. Common-interest problems require **collaboration** and monitoring because actors have incentives to defect. Common-aversion problems require **coordination** because actors mainly need convergent expectations to avoid a mutually bad outcome. For collective security, this distinction matters: some problems need enforcement against cheating, while others need rules that align expectations.

**Gourevitch:** institutions solve problems, but they also create stakes. Once rules and procedures shape outcomes, actors try to change those rules. This is the **governance problem**: institutions that manage conflict can become the object of conflict. Strong institutions channel governance disputes through rules; weak institutions leave actors to use material power, persuasion, leadership, or violence. Collective security therefore depends not only on whether actors want order, but whether the institutions of order are trusted, enforceable, and worth preserving.

## Kydd & Walter: Five Terrorism Strategies

Kydd and Walter's five strategies are all **costly signals under uncertainty**, but they differ in the audience and belief being manipulated:

| Strategy | Plain-English logic | Exam distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Attrition** | Make the target government believe the policy is too costly to continue. | Signals **resolve and cost tolerance** to the enemy: "we can keep hurting you until concession is cheaper." |
| **Intimidation** | Make civilians believe the group can punish disobedience and the state cannot protect them. | Signals **local control** to the population: "obey us because the state cannot shield you." |
| **Provocation** | Goad the state into indiscriminate retaliation that harms civilians and radicalizes the group's constituency. | Signals the **enemy's brutality or unreliability** to the domestic audience: "the state is your real enemy." |
| **Spoiling** | Attack during peace efforts to make the target doubt moderates can deliver or enforce a deal. | Signals **moderate weakness or untrustworthiness** to the enemy: "do not trust their side to keep peace." |
| **Outbidding** | Use violence to prove greater commitment than rival groups competing for the same supporters. | Signals **zeal and resolve** to the group's own constituency: "we are the true defenders, not the sellouts." |

Fast diagnostic: if the violence is meant to exhaust the enemy, think **attrition**; to control civilians, **intimidation**; to trigger overreaction, **provocation**; to wreck a peace process, **spoiling**; to beat rival factions for support, **outbidding**.

## Mechanisms To Bring To Class

- **Costly signaling:** terrorist violence is meant to change beliefs, not just cause damage. The signal works because cheap talk is not credible under conflict.
- **Audience split:** terrorists often signal to two audiences at once: the target government and their own constituency or rivals.
- **Strategy-specific response:** attrition calls for reducing costs and hardening targets; intimidation calls for restoring territorial control and protection; provocation calls for discriminating force; spoiling calls for trust-building between moderates; outbidding calls for validating nonviolent alternatives.
- **Regime logic:** collective security is easier when actors can jointly decide, monitor, and coordinate expectations rather than act independently under fear.
- **Governance contestation:** rules are not neutral background conditions. If rules distribute power, actors will fight over them.

## One Clean Synthesis

The week links terrorism to collective security through the same strategic-choice grammar: **actors use violence, rules, and institutions to alter other actors' expectations and choice sets**. Kydd and Walter show how terrorist groups exploit uncertainty to make stronger actors react in useful ways. Stein explains why states sometimes escape independent self-help by creating regimes that solve coordination or collaboration problems. Gourevitch warns that those regimes do not end politics; they relocate politics into struggles over governance rules. Put together: terrorism tests whether a state or international order can respond without becoming the terrorist's instrument, while collective security tests whether actors can build institutions strong enough to coordinate restraint but flexible enough to survive contestation.

## Good Discussion Questions

1. Which of Kydd and Walter's five strategies best explains terrorism aimed at democracies: attrition, provocation, or outbidding?
2. Is counterterrorism mainly a force problem, an information problem, or an institutional-governance problem?
3. In Stein's terms, is collective security more like a collaboration problem, where cheating must be monitored, or a coordination problem, where actors need convergent expectations?
4. If institutions become objects of political struggle, as Gourevitch argues, can collective security ever be stable, or is it always provisional?
