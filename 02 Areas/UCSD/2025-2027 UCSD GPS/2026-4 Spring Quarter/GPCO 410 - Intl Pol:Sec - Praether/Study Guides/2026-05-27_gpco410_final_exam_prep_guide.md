## Part 1: The Core Grading Engine — The 6-Slot Strategic Choice Screen

To secure the curved B+ median or higher, every essay response must be written in the **strategic choice grammar** that TA Ali Abdirisak and Professor Praether reward. Do not write a generic historical narrative. Structure your answers by walking through these six slots explicitly:

1. **Actors:** Name the explicit strategic actors (prefer individuals like *Aung San Suu Kyi* and *Min Aung Hlaing*, or *Bush* and *Saddam Hussein*, over vague collectives like "the NLD" or "the US").
2. **Preferences:** Define the actor's ordinal preference rankings (e.g., first preference, fallback, worst-case outcome). Do not infer preferences solely from the observed outcome (the "preference-strategy conflation trap").
3. **Strategy Set (including unchosen alternatives):** Enumerate the complete choice set available to each actor. You *must* state at least one plausible alternative strategy they did *not* choose and explain exactly why it was rejected (e.g., dominated by expected payoff, ruled out by beliefs).
4. **Beliefs:** Detail what each actor believed about the other's resolve, capabilities, and likely responses. This is where information problems and miscalculations enter the strategic model.
5. **Expected Payoffs:** Explain how actors calculated the net value of their choices, factoring in the costs of conflict vs. concessions.
6. **The Dedicated "Why No Deal" Beat:** Dedicate at least one full paragraph to explaining why a negotiated settlement failed. Propose the exact counterfactual terms of a bargain that would theoretically lie within the bargaining range, and explain the precise mechanism (e.g., Powell's Type 2 commitment problem, demobilization vulnerability) that made that bargain non-signable or non-credible.

## Part 2: Blue Memo Expansion Strategy (Iraq War 2003 & Powell's Commitment Problems)

### 1. Theoretical Deepening: Powell (2006)
*   **The Unifying Insight:** Do *not* say Powell offers "three named types of commitment problems." Powell's contribution is showing that **one unifying mechanism**—the structural inability to make credible intertemporal commitments when the distribution of power is shifting—explains rationalist war under complete information, with divisible stakes, and between rational actors.
*   **Three Dynamics of the Master Mechanism:**
    1.  *Preventive War:* A temporarily strong, declining actor attacks a rising actor to close a closing window of military advantage before the balance tips.
    2.  *Bargaining over Power:* Concessions are endogenous. The rising actor cannot credibly commit to not using its newly acquired power-shifting assets to demand further concessions in the future.
    3.  *First-Strike Advantage:* Offensive-dominant technology makes peaceful equilibria non-self-enforcing, forcing preemption in a crisis.
*   **Precision Vocabulary:** *Dynamic inconsistency*, *bargaining surplus*, *inefficiency puzzle*, *anticipated power shift*, *non-fungibility of concessions*, *intertemporal credibility*.

### 2. Case Application: Bush vs. Hussein (2003)
*   **Actors:** President George W. Bush (and hawkish advisors) vs. President Saddam Hussein.
*   **Preferences:** 
    *   *Bush:* $P_1$ = Disarmed Iraq with no WMD capability; $P_2$ = Regime change via war; $P_3$ = Status quo with unverified Iraqi containment (worst case).
    *   *Hussein:* $P_1$ = Regime survival with regional deterrent intact; $P_2$ = Regime survival with full disarmament; $P_3$ = Deposition/regime collapse via war (worst case).
*   **Unchosen Strategies & Rejection Logic:**
    *   *Hussein did not choose transparent, immediate disarmament in 2002.* Why? Because doing so would collapse his regional deterrent against Iran, exposing the regime to external vulnerability, and signaling internal weakness that could trigger a domestic coup.
    *   *Bush did not choose continued UN inspections/sanctions.* Why? Because post-9/11, the preemption doctrine raised the expected cost of being wrong about WMDs (the risk of a nuclear Iraq) to a level that far exceeded the cost of war.
*   **Saddam's Dual-Audience signaling (Putnam Clarification):** Do *not* call Saddam's US-vs-Iran dilemma a "two-level game." A two-level game is domestic-vs-international. Saddam's strategy was **dual-audience signaling** (or multi-audience signaling) to two external actors: signaling to the US/UN that he had no WMDs (to lift sanctions) while signaling to Iran/local rivals that he *did* have WMDs (to deter invasion).

### 3. The Counterfactual Deal & Bargaining Breakdown
*   **The Deal Terms:** A four-term package: (1) Hussein grants unlimited, immediate, unannounced access to all presidential palaces and military sites; (2) US lifts all economic sanctions; (3) US guarantees no military invasion; (4) UN monitors establish permanent border bases.
*   **Why It Wasn't Signed (Powell's Type 2 Mechanism):** Verification was itself a **power-shifting concession**. If Hussein allowed absolute transparency, he would reveal his military weakness (no WMDs), stripping him of his Iranian deterrent and domestic coup-proofing shield. Once disarmed and weak, Hussein could not prevent Bush from exploiting that weakness to demand regime change anyway. No Bush pledge could bind future U.S. decision cycles under the preemption doctrine. Because the concession itself shifted future bargaining power, the deal was non-credible.

## Part 3: Orange Memo Expansion Strategy (Myanmar Democratization & Cederman's Transition Risk)

### 1. Theoretical Deepening: Cederman, Hug, and Krebs (2010)
*   **Quantitative Spine:** Democratization does *not* make states safe in the short term. Transition periods themselves raise civil war risk (**2.77 times higher** during regime change compared to stable periods).
*   **The Critical Distinction:** Democratization disproportionately increases the risk of **governmental conflicts** (fights over who controls the national state), *not* **territorial conflicts** (fights over regional autonomy or secession).
*   **The Mechanism:** *Mobilization-Institutionalization Lag* (Huntingtonian logic). Democratization opens political participation and mobilizes new groups faster than weak state institutions can settle and absorb the distributional struggle over national authority.

### 2. Case Application: Aung San Suu Kyi vs. Min Aung Hlaing (2010–2021)
*   **Actors:** State Counsellor Aung San Suu Kyi (civilian NLD leader) vs. Senior General Min Aung Hlaing (Commander-in-Chief of the Tatmadaw).
*   **Preferences:**
    *   *Suu Kyi:* $P_1$ = Full civilian supremacy under a reformed constitution; $P_2$ = Co-governance under the 2008 constitutional limits; $P_3$ = Autocratic military reversal (worst case).
    *   *Min Aung Hlaing:* $P_1$ = Permanent military autonomy, reserved security portfolios, and final veto; $P_2$ = Managed democracy with elections that validate military dominance; $P_3$ = Loss of the military veto and civilian control over the Tatmadaw (worst case).
*   **Unchosen Strategies & Rejection Logic:**
    *   *The Tatmadaw did not choose to accept the 2020 landslide election.* Why? The NLD's massive victory eroded the military's electoral vehicle (USDP) and signaled that managed democracy would eventually destroy the military's veto. The military rejected the fallback of passive co-governance because its expected payoff was a slow slide to subordination.
    *   *Aung San Suu Kyi did not choose to build an armed civilian resistance before 2021.* Why? She prioritized international legitimacy and legalistic reform, believing that military-provoked violence would alienate moderate backers and destroy the transition's progress.
*   **The 2008 Constitution Veto (Gourevitch Institutional Rules):** The 2008 Constitution mandated that any amendment required **more than 75%** of parliamentary votes, while reserving **25% of seats** for unelected military officers (Article 436). This institutional rule gave the military a literal, permanent veto over the rules of the game.

### 3. The Counterfactual Deal & Bargaining Breakdown
*   **The Deal Terms:** A compromise package: (1) NLD pledges to preserve Article 436 (protecting the military veto) and grants permanent immunity to Senior General Min Aung Hlaing; (2) Tatmadaw permits civilian control over economic ministries and lifts some restrictions on political speech; (3) Both sides jointly govern without electoral updates for ten years.
*   **Why It Wasn't Signed (Powell/Walter Bargaining Failure):** The NLD's electoral legitimacy was a dynamic asset. Once Suu Kyi won landslides in 2015 and 2020, she could not credibly commit to not using that legitimacy to demand the removal of the military veto in the future. Conversely, the Tatmadaw could not credibly commit to not using its monopoly on physical force to depose the civilians if reforms went too far. The **anticipated shift in power** (electoral dominance eventually eroding the military's veto) exceeded the bargaining surplus. The military preempted with the 2021 coup.

### 4. Late-Quarter Dialogue: Walter Ch. 2 and Lake Ch. 1
*   **Walter's Demobilization Vulnerability:** Once the coup occurred, the resistance (NUG and PDFs) could not demobilize or disarm because doing so would expose them to immediate Tatmadaw violence. Without an external, third-party guarantor (like the UN or an armed coalition) to enforce a peace deal and protect disarmed actors, a negotiated settlement is structurally impossible. The parties are locked in civil war.
*   **Lake's Statebuilding Framework:** Myanmar represents a **Predatory State** that failed to transition to a **Consolidated State** (Lake Ch. 1, Fig 1.1). The Tatmadaw lacks domestic legitimacy, relying on extreme coercion and divide-and-conquer strategies (e.g., exploiting the Rohingya crisis to rally nationalist support). Because citizens have no relationally specific assets vested in the military junta, the regime is fragile, holding power purely through domestic terror while the state itself remains deeply factionalized.

## Part 4: Purple Memo Expansion Strategy (NATO Northern Expansion & Stein's Regimes)

### 1. Theoretical Deepening: Stein (1982) and Gourevitch (1999)
*   **Regime Definition:** A regime is not merely an international organization; it exists when patterned state behavior shifts from fully independent decision-making to **joint decision-making** (p. 299).
*   **Stein's Two Dilemmas:**
    1.  *Dilemma of Common Aversions (Coordination):* Actors do not share the same ideal outcome, but they all want to avoid a mutually disastrous outcome. It requires **coordination** to align expectations (e.g., standardizing military technology, avoiding accidental escalation). Once coordinated, there is no incentive to defect.
    2.  *Dilemma of Common Interests (Collaboration):* Actors share a common interest in a cooperative outcome, but each retains an incentive to cheat or free-ride. It requires **collaboration** (monitoring, enforcement, and cost-sharing) because the cooperative equilibrium is unstable.
*   **Gourevitch's Governance Problem:** Institutions are never neutral. Because rules distribute power and shape outcomes, **actors will fight over the rules themselves**. The governance of an institution is a constant site of political contestation.

### 2. Case Application: Finland/Sweden Accession and Turkey's Veto (2022–2024)
*   **Actors:** Applicant states (Finland and Sweden); existing NATO allies (led by the US/Biden as a supporting ratifier); process broker (Jens Stoltenberg); bargaining member (Turkey/Erdogan).
*   **Preferences:**
    *   *Finland/Sweden:* $P_1$ = NATO integration with Article 5 protection; $P_2$ = Armed non-alignment under U.S. bilateral security pacts; $P_3$ = Isolated non-alignment under Russian threat (worst case).
    *   *Turkey:* $P_1$ = NATO expansion *plus* concession on Kurdish extraditions and arms embargoes; $P_2$ = Maintain NATO status quo (block expansion); $P_3$ = Expand NATO without concessions (worst case).
*   **Unchosen Strategies & Rejection Logic:**
    *   *Turkey did not choose to unilaterally veto Finland and Sweden permanently.* Why? Because doing so would alienate the U.S. and other allies, risking exclusion from defense procurement (F-16s) and weakening the overall alliance deterrent against Russia. Permanent obstruction was dominated by the payoff of a negotiated concession.
    *   *Finland and Sweden did not choose to maintain their long-standing non-alignment after February 24, 2022.* Why? Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine changed the security payoff of staying outside NATO. Non-alignment, which previously preserved strategic flexibility, was now read as an invitation to Russian aggression.
*   **The Consensus Rule (Gourevitch Rules in Action):** NATO's institutional rule requiring **unanimous consensus** for new accessions gave Turkey a high-leverage veto. Turkey exploited this rule to shift the distributive outcomes of the regime, extracting policy concessions (the June 2022 Madrid Trilateral Memorandum) regarding Kurdish activities and arms export bans before ratifying accession.

### 3. Late-Quarter Dialogue: Costly Signaling & Statebuilding
*   **Kydd & Walter's Costly Signaling:** Turkey's delay and Finland/Sweden's rapid application operated as costly signals.
    *   *Finland/Sweden:* Applying to NATO was a **costly signal of resolve** to Russia. It meant sacrificing decades of diplomatic neutrality, accepting Article 5 obligations, and permanently aligning with the West.
    *   *Turkey:* Erdogan's public objections were a **spoiling or attrition signal** to Sweden, raising the cost of entry until Sweden changed its domestic laws regarding Kurdish organizations.
*   **Lake's Vested Interests:** The accession of Finland and Sweden is a classic example of **vesting relationally specific assets** (Lake Ch. 1). By integrating their military commands, sharing intelligence, and aligning defense plans with NATO, Finland and Sweden are locking themselves into the Western security regime. Their assets (military infrastructure, geographic airspace) are now specific to NATO, making exit or retreat back into neutrality highly costly and self-enforcing.
