# Murray, "The West at War, 1914-1918": Turkish / Ottoman Campaign Focus

**Reading focus:** Williamson A. Murray, "The West at War, 1914-1918," in *The Cambridge History of Warfare*, pp. 278-313

## BLUF

In the WWI Murray reading, "the Turkish campaign" should be read first as the **Dardanelles / Gallipoli campaign of 1915**. Murray presents it as one of the war's best strategic ideas and one of its worst operational executions: knocking Ottoman Turkey out of the war could have reopened supply lines to Russia, helped Serbia, influenced the Balkan states, and pressured Austria-Hungary, but poor amphibious preparation, weak intelligence, slow command, Turkish defensive firepower, and Mustafa Kemal's local initiative turned the campaign into a costly Allied failure (Murray, 2005, pp. 288-291).

## What Counts As Turkish / Ottoman In This Reading

Murray's WWI chapter is mostly about the European war, but the Ottoman front matters at several pressure points:

- **October 1914:** Ottoman attacks against Russia in the Caucasus and Black Sea added strain to an already overextended Russian army and logistics system (Murray, 2005, p. 286).
- **1915:** The Dardanelles/Gallipoli campaign became the major Allied attempt to use the Ottoman theater to break the strategic deadlock.
- **Balkans after Gallipoli:** Allied failure helped Bulgaria join the Central Powers, which enabled German and Austrian forces to eliminate Serbia and tighten the Central Powers' hold over the Balkans (Murray, 2005, p. 291).
- **1918:** Turkey, exhausted by four years of war, made peace before Germany's final collapse. Murray treats this as part of the wider unraveling of Germany's coalition (Murray, 2005, p. 310).

Megiddo is relevant to the May 11 class context because it was on the presentation schedule and is an Ottoman-front 1918 case, but Murray's assigned chapter does not develop Megiddo in detail. The reading's main Turkish/Ottoman campaign content is Gallipoli/Dardanelles.

## Operational Picture: Dardanelles / Gallipoli

The Allied strategic logic was ambitious and not stupid. Churchill argued that forcing the Dardanelles could remove Turkey from the war, open sea communications to Russia, draw Romania and Bulgaria toward the Allies, support Serbia, and create a third front against Austria-Hungary (Murray, 2005, p. 288). In a war stuck in trench attrition, this was an attempt to restore maneuver at the strategic level.

The naval plan failed first. The Royal Navy tried to force the straits with older battleships, expecting that a breakthrough into the Sea of Marmara could threaten Constantinople and collapse Turkey's will. Turkish shore batteries and mines stopped the attempt (Murray, 2005, pp. 289-290). That forced the Allies into a land campaign they had not properly prepared.

The landing plan then repeated the same problem on land. Murray emphasizes that the ground forces had little amphibious training and that planners had not answered basic operational questions: water, roads, terrain, likely fighting conditions, and Turkish defensive strengths (Murray, 2005, p. 290). The Allies could get ashore, but could not convert lodgments into control of the decisive heights.

The Turkish defense succeeded through terrain, firepower, and rapid local leadership. Turkish machine gunners punished the British landing at the tip of the peninsula. At Anzac Cove, the Anzacs landed in the wrong area and moved too slowly toward the heights. Mustafa Kemal recognized the key terrain and rushed reinforcements forward before the attackers could seize it (Murray, 2005, p. 290).

By August, the campaign had become a smaller version of the Western Front problem: defenders on high ground plus modern weapons made movement nearly impossible. The Suvla Bay landing offered another chance, but command confusion and delay let the Turks prepare. The British withdrew in winter after nearly half a million Allied troops had fought there and roughly half became casualties (Murray, 2005, pp. 290-291).

## Why It Matters In Murray's WWI Argument

Gallipoli is Murray's clearest example of a campaign where strategic imagination outran operational competence. The Western Front was locked in attrition, and the Dardanelles idea tried to break the war open indirectly. But the Allies did not solve the concrete mechanics: amphibious operations, terrain, enemy defenses, command clarity, logistics, and exploitation.

The campaign also shows that the Ottoman Empire was not merely a weak peripheral actor. Turkish resistance shaped coalition strategy. By surviving Gallipoli, Turkey helped keep Russia isolated, encouraged Bulgaria's alignment with the Central Powers, and contributed to Serbia's destruction. The Ottoman front therefore mattered not because it decided the war alone, but because it affected the pressure on Russia, the Balkans, and the durability of Germany's coalition.

For Thomas's course, the point is not "Turkey beat Britain" in a simple sense. The point is that modern war punishes forces that confuse a clever strategic idea with an executable campaign. Gallipoli makes the practical layers visible: intelligence, local initiative, topography, firepower, amphibious skill, logistics, and command.

## Thomas Theme Hooks

- **Mass:** Allied troop numbers became trapped in narrow landing zones. Mass without room, roads, water, and operational momentum became a casualty generator.
- **Mobility:** Amphibious movement created entry, not maneuver. The decisive race was inland to the heights, and the Turks won it.
- **Adaptability:** Mustafa Kemal adapted faster at the local level than the Allied command system did at the operational level.
- **Supply chains / logistics:** Murray stresses unanswered basics: water, roads, terrain, and sustainment. This is logistics before the shooting starts.
- **Operational leadership:** Churchill's strategic imagination was not matched by Hamilton's and Stopford's execution. Kemal is the leadership counterexample.
- **Offense vs. defense:** Modern defensive firepower plus high ground dominated. Gallipoli was the Western Front logic translated into an amphibious theater.

## Ottoman-Front Context To Remember

- **Caucasus and Black Sea:** Ottoman action against Russia in 1914 added pressure to Russia's already strained logistics and army structure.
- **Dardanelles / Gallipoli:** The main Turkish campaign in Murray's text; strategic logic strong, operational execution poor.
- **Bulgaria and Serbia:** Gallipoli's failure helped make the Balkan situation worse for the Allies by encouraging Bulgaria to join the Central Powers.
- **1918 collapse:** Turkey's peace in September/October 1918 is part of the coalition-collapse story that leaves Germany with no strategic cards.
- **Megiddo:** Useful class-adjacent Ottoman-front case, but not the main Murray-text case. If Thomas cues Megiddo, use it as a late-war contrast in operational exploitation.

## Quiz / Watch Points

- If asked "what was the Turkish campaign?", answer **Dardanelles / Gallipoli, 1915**, then add that Megiddo is the later Ottoman-front presentation case.
- Do not describe Gallipoli as a dumb idea. Murray's sharper point is that it was a strategically promising idea ruined by operational and tactical failure.
- Know the failure chain: naval mines and shore batteries; weak amphibious preparation; poor terrain and logistics knowledge; slow Allied movement to the heights; Mustafa Kemal's rapid reinforcement; Suvla Bay hesitation; winter withdrawal.
- Tie Gallipoli to the larger WWI reading: it is an indirect attempt to escape attrition, but it reproduces the same offense-defense problem under different geography.
- Remember the coalition consequence: failure at Gallipoli helped Bulgaria join the Central Powers and helped Germany/Austria eliminate Serbia.
