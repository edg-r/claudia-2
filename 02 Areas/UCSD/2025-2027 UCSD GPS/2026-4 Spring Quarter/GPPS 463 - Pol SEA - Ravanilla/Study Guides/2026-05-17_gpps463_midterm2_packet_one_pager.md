## 1. Asian Miracle: Growth Is Real, but the Mechanism Matters

**Lecture fit:** LD8 asks why only some countries experienced the Asian Miracle; LD9 asks how Singapore did it. The shared lecture move is growth accounting: separate more inputs from better use of inputs (Ravanilla, 2026a, 2026b).

**Core argument:** Krugman does not deny East Asian growth. He argues that much of it came from mobilizing capital, labor, education, savings, and work hours rather than from a mysterious productivity breakthrough. That makes the "miracle" impressive but not automatically permanent, because input-driven growth faces diminishing returns (Krugman, 1994).

**Mechanism chain:** high savings and labor mobilization -> capital accumulation and export production -> rapid output growth -> eventual pressure for productivity gains once easy input gains slow.

**Country logic:** Thailand is the lighter-touch case. It avoided formal colonization, kept a centralized bureaucracy and military-monarchical elite, opened early through the Bowring Treaty, and relied heavily on Chinese-Thai entrepreneurial networks, household savings, FDI, rural labor, and export growth. Singapore is the state-capacity case. Its PAP state created comparative advantage through forced savings, wage coordination, labor discipline, housing, industrial estates, MNE attraction, and repeated upgrading from low-value manufacturing to services and regional production networks (Ravanilla, 2026a, 2026b).

**Likely comparison points:** Thailand shows that a classic developmental state is not necessary for fast growth; Singapore shows that market openness and heavy state intervention can coexist. Krugman is the foil for both: even when the state or entrepreneurs are impressive, ask whether growth came from productivity or mobilized inputs.

## 2. Singapore: State-Created Comparative Advantage

**Lecture fit:** LD9 treats Singapore as the strongest Midterm 2 case for active industrial policy. The exam trap is calling Singapore laissez-faire just because it was open to trade and FDI (Ravanilla, 2026b).

**Core argument:** Singapore used markets, but it did not simply trust markets. The state changed the cost structure facing firms and workers. CPF mobilized savings; HDB stabilized labor and nation-building; EDB attracted multinational firms; Jurong created industrial space; NTUC and the National Wages Council coordinated labor and wages; the SIJORI Growth Triangle extended Singapore's economy beyond its small territory.

**Mechanism chain:** small vulnerable city-state -> PAP administrative capacity -> savings, labor peace, infrastructure, FDI platform -> export-oriented manufacturing -> wage and sectoral upgrading -> services, finance, logistics, and external economy.

**Exam-ready line:** Singapore confirms Krugman only halfway. Yes, growth required enormous input mobilization. But the political question is why Singapore could mobilize inputs, discipline labor, attract foreign capital, and upgrade sectors more coherently than most Southeast Asian states.

**Limits:** The model is hard to replicate because Singapore had unusual city-state scale, port geography, PAP dominance, disciplined bureaucracy, and willingness to trade political contestation for technocratic coordination.

## 3. Asian Financial Crisis: Same Shock, Different Institutional Filters

**Lecture fit:** LD10 gives the basic AFC economics, then moves quickly to politics. Hot money, dollar pegs, weak regulation, non-performing loans, crony lending, and contagion explain vulnerability, but institutions explain variation in response (Ravanilla, 2026c).

**Core arguments:** Wade says the crisis cannot be reduced to Asian cronyism or domestic governance failure. Global financial liberalization, volatile capital flows, core-economy policy, and the post-Bretton Woods financial system created the outer machinery of vulnerability (Wade, 2000). MacIntyre then explains why domestic political systems responded differently: too many veto players create policy rigidity, while too few create policy volatility. Investors want the middle, enough constraint for credible commitment and enough flexibility for decisive action (MacIntyre, 2001).

**Mechanism chain:** open capital accounts and short-term inflows -> asset and credit boom -> confidence shock and capital flight -> need for decisive but credible reform -> domestic veto structure shapes recovery.

**Thailand vs. Philippines:** Hicken's key paradox is that Thailand was hit harder but reformed more; the Philippines was hit less hard and therefore had less pressure to overhaul weak institutions. Thailand's 1997 Constitution reduced party fragmentation and strengthened prime-ministerial power, helping Thai Rak Thai turn crisis politics into programmatic, nationally coordinated policy. The same concentration later created conflict with informal veto players, including courts, bureaucracy, monarchy-linked networks, military actors, and urban protest movements (Hicken, 2006, 2008). The Philippines avoided the worst initial crisis, but that mildness validated partial reforms and left dynastic politics, weak revenue, GOCC liabilities, oligarchic business-politics ties, and later presidential instability unresolved (Hicken, 2008).

**Likely comparison points:** Wade explains why the region became vulnerable; MacIntyre explains why policy credibility and decisiveness varied; Hicken explains why crisis severity can produce reform momentum. The cleanest short-answer contrast is "Thailand: worse shock, stronger reform pressure; Philippines: milder shock, weaker reform pressure."

## 4. Vietnam and China: Authoritarianism Is Not One Institution

**Lecture fit:** LD11 asks how Vietnam is doing under communism, but the real exam concept is internal institutional variation inside single-party regimes (Ravanilla, 2026d).

**Core argument:** Malesky, Abrami, and Zheng argue that Vietnam has lower inequality than China not because it is democratic, richer, less globalized, or less ethnically complex. Vietnam's internal party institutions are broader and more competitive. Its Central Committee has greater power relative to the Politburo, includes provincial and mass-organization voices, and can check top leaders. China's key decision-making is more concentrated in the Politburo and Politburo Standing Committee (Malesky et al., 2011).

**Mechanism chain:** broader internal party coalition -> leaders must account for more provinces and social constituencies -> larger equalization transfers and public-service spending -> lower inequality growth than China.

**Likely comparison points:** Do not write "communist regimes redistribute." The sharper answer is that authoritarian institutions vary: Vietnam's broader vertical checks produce more equalizing transfers, while China's concentrated elite structure enables faster concentration of gains. This also connects back to MacIntyre: institutional concentration can improve decisiveness, but it can also weaken accountability.
