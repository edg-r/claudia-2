## Lecture Day 8: Why Did Only Some Countries Experience the Asian Miracle?

### Core Takeaway

Lecture Day 8 connects the end of colonial rule to the later puzzle of uneven economic growth. Southeast Asian countries entered independence with different elite structures, nationalist movements, administrative capacities, and social cleavages because colonialism usually reworked older elites rather than replacing them. World War II then shattered European prestige, accelerated nationalist mobilization, and made decolonization unavoidable, but the postwar states that emerged varied sharply in violence, institutional capacity, and nation-building success. The lecture then turns to the "Asian Miracle" and asks why Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia grew much faster than laggards such as the Philippines, Burma, Laos, Cambodia, and Timor-Leste. Thailand is the main case: its non-colonized status preserved a centralized bureaucracy and military-monarchical elite structure, while Chinese entrepreneurs, household savings, FDI, and later improvements in total factor productivity helped fuel market-oriented growth. Krugman's input-mobilization argument is important here because it cautions against treating high growth as mysterious or infinitely sustainable (Krugman, 1994).

### Key Concepts and Terms

- Colonial intermediaries: local elites, Chinese and Indian commercial intermediaries, and Western-educated intelligentsia who linked colonial states to society.
- Principalia and ilustrados: Philippine elite groups that show how nationalist leadership often grew out of older colonial-era status networks.
- Priyayi: Javanese aristocratic-bureaucratic elites used by Dutch rule in Indonesia.
- Mandarin or scholar-gentry: Vietnamese elite layer partly preserved under French rule and later challenged by French-educated nationalists.
- Nascent nationalism: pre-WWII anti-colonial identity built around an "us" against the colonizer, but still uneven and incomplete.
- Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere: Japanese ideological packaging of occupation as Asian liberation from Western rule.
- Collaboration and resistance: wartime political choices that shaped postwar legitimacy, especially in Indonesia, Burma, the Philippines, and Vietnam.
- Point of no return: the Japanese occupation destroyed the myth of European superiority and made a full return to prewar colonial dominance impossible.
- Decolonization pathways: violent independence struggles, peaceful transitions, and varied degrees of continued colonial presence.
- Asian Miracle: sustained 7-8 percent growth in several East and Southeast Asian economies from the 1960s through the 1990s.
- Growth accounting: separating growth from inputs, capital and labor, versus efficiency, total factor productivity.
- Cobb-Douglas production function: Y = AK^alpha L^beta, where A is total factor productivity, K is capital, and L is labor.
- Total Factor Productivity (TFP): the efficiency with which capital and labor are used.
- Bowring Treaty: 1855 treaty opening Siam to British trade, limiting royal taxation, and helping preserve formal independence while creating foreign economic constraints.
- Market-oriented development: Thailand's relatively low-intervention growth path compared with more state-led cases such as Singapore.

### Countries and Cases

- Thailand: formal independence, centralized bureaucracy, Bowring Treaty, Chinese entrepreneurial class, military-bureaucratic politics, uneven urban-rural distribution.
- Philippines: principalia and ilustrado roots, elite collaboration during Japanese occupation, 1946 independence, Hukbalahap insurgency.
- Indonesia: Sukarno and Hatta, Japanese-enabled nationalist organizing, 1945 declaration, Dutch return, armed independence struggle, "Unity in Diversity."
- Burma: weakened royal elite, student-monk-lawyer nationalist networks, Burma Independence Army, Aung San, post-1948 ideological and ethnic conflict.
- Vietnam: French colonial rule, Viet Minh, Ho Chi Minh, 1945 declaration, First and Second Indochina Wars, unification in 1975.
- Malaya, Singapore, and Brunei: British return, Emergency, Federation of Malaysia, Singapore's expulsion and independence in 1965, Brunei's delayed independence.
- Cambodia and Laos: weaker nationalist mobilization, French return, independence under monarchical-conservative arrangements.
- Japan: occupation as the shock that destroyed European colonial legitimacy.

### Named Authors and Readings Linked to the Lecture

- Paul Krugman, "The Myth of Asia's Miracle": The lecture's growth-accounting framework tracks Krugman's claim that much East Asian growth came from extraordinary resource mobilization, especially savings, capital accumulation, labor-force expansion, education, and longer work hours, rather than a unique formula for permanently superior productivity growth (Krugman, 1994).

### Likely Exam Angles

- Explain why WWII was a turning point for Southeast Asian decolonization, not just another episode of colonial conflict.
- Compare countries where older elites survived colonialism with countries where colonialism weakened older elites.
- Connect wartime collaboration or resistance to postwar political legitimacy.
- Distinguish growth through more inputs from growth through higher productivity.
- Explain why Thailand's formal independence was both an advantage and a constraint.
- Use Thailand to connect centralized bureaucracy, Chinese entrepreneurship, state-business bargains, and later inequality.
- Avoid treating the Asian Miracle as one regional story; the lecture is about variation.

### Study Questions

1. How did colonial rule usually transform, rather than abolish, older Southeast Asian elites?
2. Why did the Japanese occupation make a return to prewar colonial dominance politically impossible?
3. Why did Indonesia and Vietnam face violent independence struggles while Malaysia, Singapore, Cambodia, Laos, Brunei, and the Philippines experienced more peaceful or negotiated transitions?
4. What does growth accounting add to the study of the Asian Miracle?
5. In Krugman's argument, why is input-driven growth vulnerable to diminishing returns?
6. How did Thailand's non-colonized status shape its bureaucracy, infrastructure, elite politics, and later growth model?
7. Why did Thailand's growth produce uneven distribution between urban and rural populations and between capitalists and laborers?

## Lecture Day 9: Asian Miracle, How Did Singapore Do It?

### Core Takeaway

Lecture Day 9 uses Singapore to show that the Asian Miracle was not simply a free-market story. Singapore shared broad success conditions with other high-growth economies, including macroeconomic orthodoxy, trade openness, and some equity, but it also relied on an unusually active state that deliberately shaped costs, savings, labor, finance, housing, infrastructure, FDI, and sectoral upgrading. The state did not replace markets; it used markets while pushing firms and workers through successive phases of structural transformation. This case complicates the Washington Consensus reading of development: Thailand is closer to a minimal-state, entrepreneur-led case, while Singapore is a developmental administrative state that distorted prices to create comparative advantage. Krugman is still relevant because Singapore's growth required massive input mobilization, but the lecture asks a further political question: how did the state mobilize those inputs so effectively (Krugman, 1994)?

### Key Concepts and Terms

- Macroeconomic orthodoxy: price stability, fiscal discipline, external balance, and credible exchange-rate management.
- Trade openness: integration into global markets through exports, FDI, and international production chains.
- Equity: enough distributional inclusion to sustain social order and development legitimacy.
- Washington Consensus: market-oriented development package emphasizing limited government intervention, public goods, fiscal discipline, taxes, and monetary policy.
- Developmental state: a state that actively coordinates industrial upgrading and structural transformation.
- Price distortion for comparative advantage: deliberate state manipulation of wages, finance, infrastructure, and incentives to move the economy into targeted sectors.
- Export-Oriented Industrialization (EOI): export-led strategy using global markets rather than only import substitution.
- Entrepot trade: Singapore's historical role as a port and transshipment economy.
- NTUC: National Trades Union Congress; labor control and labor peace.
- NWC: National Wages Council; wage coordination, including corrective wage policy.
- DBS: Development Bank of Singapore; state-linked finance.
- NOL: Neptune Orient Lines; logistics and shipping capacity.
- HDB: Housing Development Board; housing as infrastructure, social policy, and nation-building.
- EDB: Economic Development Board; FDI attraction and industrial estates.
- CPF and POSB: compulsory savings and postal savings institutions used for domestic capital mobilization.
- Jurong Industrial Estate: state-built industrial infrastructure for manufacturing.
- Corrective wage policy: late-1970s wage increases designed to push firms out of low-value production.
- GIC: Government of Singapore Investment Corporation; sovereign wealth management.
- SIJORI Growth Triangle: Singapore-Johor-Riau regional production network.
- Economic Review Committee (ERC): post-AFC review that pushed diversification.
- Multiracialism: nation-building strategy using housing, education, national service, language, symbols, and public holidays to forge cohesion.

### Countries and Cases

- Singapore: four development phases from low-value EOI to higher-value manufacturing, services, external economy, and post-AFC diversification.
- Malaysia: Singapore's separation in 1965, Bumiputra policy, ethnic and political tensions, Johor in the SIJORI triangle.
- Indonesia: Riau in the SIJORI triangle; Suharto as a growth-committed authoritarian counterexample to simple "right leader" arguments.
- Thailand: comparison case for more market-oriented, entrepreneur-led growth.
- Philippines: Ferdinand Marcos as a counterexample showing that authoritarian rule alone does not guarantee developmental success.
- Hong Kong, South Korea, and Taiwan: other newly industrializing economies used as benchmark cases.

### Named Authors and Readings Linked to the Lecture

- Paul Krugman, "The Myth of Asia's Miracle": Krugman's argument appears directly in the lecture. He argues that East Asian growth was largely the reward for mobilizing resources, especially capital and labor, rather than evidence of an economic miracle based on exceptional productivity growth. The Singapore lecture uses that point but pushes beyond it by asking why Singapore was able to mobilize savings, labor discipline, FDI, and industrial upgrading so effectively (Krugman, 1994).

### Likely Exam Angles

- Compare Thailand's lighter-touch growth model with Singapore's active industrial policy model.
- Explain the four phases of Singapore's development and what problem each phase solved.
- Show how institutions such as HDB, CPF, NWC, EDB, and DBS were development tools, not just administrative agencies.
- Explain why Singapore's state intervention was compatible with trade openness.
- Assess whether Singapore confirms or complicates Krugman's input-mobilization argument.
- Discuss whether Singapore's model is replicable elsewhere, especially given its paternalistic and authoritarian features.
- Link economic strategy to nation-building, especially multiracialism, national service, housing, and bilingual policy.

### Study Questions

1. What did Singapore have in common with other Asian Miracle economies, and what made its state role distinctive?
2. Why did Singapore adopt export-oriented industrialization after 1965?
3. How did institutions such as CPF, HDB, NWC, DBS, and EDB help solve Singapore's early development problems?
4. Why did Singapore deliberately raise wages in the late 1970s, and how did that policy push structural transformation?
5. How did the SIJORI Growth Triangle extend Singapore's economy beyond its territorial constraints?
6. How does Krugman's argument explain Singapore's growth, and where does it leave unanswered political questions?
7. Can Singapore's developmental model be replicated without Singapore's small size, administrative capacity, and willingness to restrict civil liberties?
8. How did Singapore use nation-building policies to support economic governance?

## Lecture Day 10: Why Was the Asian Financial Crisis Unequally Felt in the Region?

### Core Takeaway

Lecture Day 10 shifts from why the Asian Miracle happened to why the Asian Financial Crisis produced uneven political and economic recovery. The lecture gives only a basic economic intuition for the crisis: speculative "hot money," weak financial regulation, hidden non-performing loans, crony capitalism, dollar pegs, capital flight, and contagion. The real focus is institutional. Governments facing crisis must be decisive enough to act, but credible enough that investors believe policy will remain stable. MacIntyre's veto-player framework explains this tradeoff: too many veto players can produce policy rigidity, while too few can produce policy volatility (MacIntyre, 2001). Thailand and the Philippines are the main comparison. Thailand was hit harder, but the crisis generated political urgency, constitutional reform, and the rise of Thai Rak Thai, which reduced fragmentation and made programmatic policy more possible, even at the cost of excluding informal veto players who later turned to protests, courts, and coups (Hicken, 2006, 2008). The Philippines was hit less severely and responded relatively quickly under Ramos, but because the crisis did not generate deep reform momentum, dynastic politics, debt, weak revenues, GOCCs, corruption, and later political crises persisted (Hicken, 2008).

### Key Concepts and Terms

- Asian Financial Crisis (AFC): 1997 regional financial crisis beginning in Thailand and spreading across East and Southeast Asia.
- Hot money: short-term speculative capital that moves quickly across borders in response to interest rates, exchange-rate expectations, and investor confidence.
- FDI versus speculative flows: long-term productive investment versus short-term financial capital.
- Pegged exchange rate: commitment to hold the local currency at a fixed rate against the U.S. dollar.
- Dollar reserves: foreign currency reserves used to defend a fixed exchange rate.
- Currency devaluation: fall in a currency's value after the peg becomes unsustainable.
- Capital flight: investors selling local assets or currencies and moving money abroad.
- Contagion: investor panic spreading from one country to others.
- Non-performing loans (NPLs): loans unlikely to be repaid, often hidden before crisis.
- Crony capitalism: politically connected lending and business protection that weaken financial discipline.
- IMF conditionality: crisis lending tied to policy reforms.
- Veto player or veto point: an actor or institution that can block policy change.
- Credible commitment: investor belief that the government will not reverse policy arbitrarily.
- Decisiveness: ability to act quickly and coherently.
- Policy rigidity: inability to change policy because veto authority is too dispersed.
- Policy volatility: wild policy swings because veto authority is too concentrated and unconstrained.
- Sweet spot: institutional middle ground that provides enough constraint for credibility and enough flexibility for action.
- MPS: multi-member plurality system; Thailand's pre-1997 lower-house electoral system.
- SMD and FPTP: single-member district and first-past-the-post rules in the Philippines.
- GOCCs: government-owned and controlled corporations, a major fiscal burden in the Philippines.
- Thai Rak Thai: Thaksin Shinawatra's party, empowered by post-1997 institutional reforms.
- Informal veto players: actors such as military, monarchy-linked networks, courts, bureaucracy, business groups, and urban middle class that may block politics outside ordinary electoral channels.

### Countries and Cases

- Thailand: crisis epicenter, pre-1997 fragmented parties and clientelism, IMF reforms, 1997 Constitution, Thai Rak Thai, Thaksin, 30-baht health scheme, farmer debt moratorium, village fund, later coups and protests.
- Philippines: milder initial crisis, Ramos response, dynastic politics, oligarchic business-politics overlap, at-large Senate, strong presidency, debt burden, weak revenue, GOCC liabilities, Estrada and Arroyo crises.
- Malaysia: crisis impact and recovery mentioned as part of the regional comparison; in MacIntyre, concentrated executive authority creates risks of volatility.
- Indonesia: severe crisis, Suharto, cronyism, and policy volatility in MacIntyre's framework.
- Hong Kong and South Korea: later spread of contagion beyond Southeast Asia.
- United States and Japan: Wade's broader argument emphasizes international capital markets and core-economy governments in creating crisis vulnerabilities (Wade, 2000).

### Named Authors and Readings Linked to the Lecture

- Andrew MacIntyre, "Institutions and Investors": The lecture's veto-player framework is based on MacIntyre. His central claim is that the distribution of veto authority shapes the policy environment investors face. Wide veto dispersion risks rigidity; tight veto concentration risks volatility; intermediate arrangements may balance credibility and flexibility (MacIntyre, 2001).
- Robert Wade, "Wheels Within Wheels": Wade is useful for the broader AFC context. He pushes against explanations that blame only Asian cronyism or the "Asian model," arguing that international capital markets, U.S. and Japanese policy, and financial openness were central causes of the crisis (Wade, 2000).
- Allen Hicken, "Politics of Economic Recovery in Thailand and the Philippines": Hicken directly supports the lecture's Thailand-Philippines comparison. Thailand's deeper crisis generated political reform momentum and later robust recovery, while the Philippines' milder crisis reduced pressure for reform and left long-term vulnerabilities unresolved (Hicken, 2008).
- Allen Hicken, "Party Fabrication": Hicken's Thai Rak Thai article explains why Thailand's 1997 constitutional reforms reduced party fragmentation, strengthened the prime minister, and created incentives that Thaksin used to build a more national, programmatic party (Hicken, 2006).

### Likely Exam Angles

- Explain the AFC with enough economic intuition to set up the political argument, but do not stop at economics.
- Use veto players to explain why crisis recovery differs across countries.
- Compare policy rigidity and policy volatility, then apply the distinction to Southeast Asian cases.
- Explain why Thailand could be hit harder yet recover more dramatically than the Philippines.
- Explain why the Philippines' milder crisis may have weakened pressure for reform.
- Discuss the intended and unintended consequences of Thailand's 1997 Constitution and Thai Rak Thai's rise.
- Connect formal institutions to informal veto players, especially why excluded actors later used courts, protests, and coups.
- Use Wade as a caveat: domestic institutions mattered for response, but global financial structures helped create the crisis.

### Study Questions

1. What economic vulnerabilities made Southeast Asian economies exposed to the 1997 crisis?
2. Why did dollar pegs and hot money make capital flight so dangerous once investor confidence fell?
3. What is a veto player, and how does the number of veto players affect credible commitment and decisiveness?
4. Why can too many veto players create policy rigidity, while too few create policy volatility?
5. Why did Thailand's severe crisis create more reform momentum than the Philippines' milder crisis?
6. How did the 1997 Thai Constitution help create the conditions for Thai Rak Thai's rise?
7. What were the benefits and costs of Thaksin becoming a more decisive veto player?
8. How does Wade's global-capital-market argument complicate an explanation focused only on domestic institutions?

## Lecture Day 11: How Is Vietnam Doing Under Communism?

### Core Takeaway

Lecture Day 11 asks why Vietnam and China, both fast-growing single-party communist regimes, produced different inequality outcomes. The puzzle is that standard explanations predict Vietnam should have had equal or greater inequality: it started poorer, was more globally exposed by exports and FDI, had somewhat greater ethnic fractionalization, and had a larger rural and agricultural population. The lecture's answer follows Malesky, Abrami, and Zheng: the difference lies inside the black box of single-party rule (Malesky et al., 2011). China concentrated power after Tiananmen through a fused leadership core and a stronger Politburo Standing Committee. Vietnam, after leadership turnover and pressure on the VCP in the late 1980s, broadened the Central Committee, separated major leadership offices, included more provincial and mass-organization voices, and made internal elections more competitive. That broader internal coalition pushed Vietnam toward larger equalization transfers, more public service spending, and lower inequality growth. The exam point is not "Vietnam is democratic"; it is that authoritarian institutions differ internally, and those differences shape policy.

### Key Concepts and Terms

- Single-party regime: authoritarian system in which one party monopolizes legal national political power.
- Kuznets hypothesis: early growth at low income levels tends to increase inequality before later equalization.
- Global economic integration: exports, FDI, and exposure to world markets as possible drivers of inequality.
- Ethnic fractionalization: diversity measure sometimes used to explain public goods and inequality outcomes.
- Fiscal arrangements: rules for revenue collection, spending, and transfers across levels of government.
- Equalization transfers: redistribution from richer to poorer provinces for education, infrastructure, social welfare, and poverty alleviation.
- Public service delivery: provision of basic goods such as education and poverty programs.
- Gini coefficient: measure of income inequality.
- CCP: Chinese Communist Party.
- VCP: Vietnamese Communist Party.
- PBSC: Politburo Standing Committee, China's concentrated top leadership body.
- Politburo: elite decision-making body, especially important in China.
- CCOM: Central Committee, the key broader decision-making body in Vietnam.
- Troika: division or fusion of top offices such as president, prime minister or premier, general secretary, and military chair.
- Paramount leader: China-style concentration of authority in a dominant leader.
- Leadership core: Chinese term signaling a leader is more than first among equals.
- Vertical checks: internal party accountability from broader bodies, lower levels, provinces, and mass organizations.
- Semicompetitive elections: controlled but meaningful internal competition for party positions.
- Inclusive authoritarian institution: non-democratic institution that still forces leaders to bargain with a broader coalition.

### Countries and Cases

- Vietnam: VCP, Central Committee of about 160 members, broader representation, competitive internal elections, separated leadership posts, equalization transfers, moderate inequality.
- China: CCP, Politburo and PBSC dominance, post-Tiananmen concentration under Jiang Zemin, later Xi Jinping as core leader, weaker internal checks, higher inequality.
- Cambodia: appears indirectly as a source of pressure on Vietnam in the late 1980s due to war and legitimacy costs.

### Named Authors and Readings Linked to the Lecture

- Edmund Malesky, Regina Abrami, and Yu Zheng, "Institutions and Inequality in Single-Party Regimes": This is the lecture's main reading. The authors argue that Vietnam's broader and more competitive elite institutions force leaders to consider a wider social and provincial coalition, producing larger equalizing transfers and less inequality than China (Malesky et al., 2011).

### Likely Exam Angles

- Explain why Vietnam-China inequality is a puzzle, not an obvious outcome.
- Rule out usual suspects: Kuznets, globalization, population size, ethnic heterogeneity, and broad regime-type labels.
- Compare China's concentrated Politburo/PBSC structure with Vietnam's broader Central Committee.
- Explain how internal party institutions shape fiscal transfers and public service delivery.
- Distinguish democracy from internal authoritarian accountability.
- Connect coalition breadth to redistribution.
- Discuss the tradeoff implied in the reading: Vietnam's architecture may privilege equality, but potentially at some cost to long-run growth efficiency.

### Study Questions

1. Why does Kuznets hypothesis fail to explain Vietnam's lower inequality relative to China?
2. Why does global economic integration predict more inequality in Vietnam, and why is that prediction wrong?
3. What institutional changes in China after Tiananmen concentrated power?
4. Why did Vietnam's late-1980s leadership transition lead to broader internal party institutions rather than stronger centralized control?
5. How does the Vietnamese Central Committee differ from China's Politburo and Politburo Standing Committee?
6. Why do broader and more competitive internal party institutions produce more equalization transfers?
7. Why is the claim not that Vietnam is democratic, but that authoritarian institutions vary internally?

## Cross-Lecture Patterns To Know

- Institutions are the course's through-line. Colonial institutions shape postwar states, developmental institutions shape growth, veto institutions shape crisis recovery, and internal party institutions shape redistribution.
- Do not flatten Southeast Asia into one regional story. The exam is likely to reward comparative mechanisms: Thailand versus Singapore, Thailand versus Philippines, Vietnam versus China, miracle cases versus laggards.
- Economic outcomes are political outcomes. Growth, crisis recovery, inequality, and industrial upgrading all depend on political coalitions, bureaucracies, and credible policy commitments.
- State capacity can help or hurt depending on institutional constraint. Singapore's strong state coordinated development; Indonesia's highly concentrated executive authority produced policy volatility in MacIntyre's crisis framework; Thailand's reduced fragmentation enabled decisive policy but also empowered Thaksin and provoked extra-constitutional resistance.
- Readings often supply the mechanism behind lecture claims. Krugman explains input-driven growth; MacIntyre explains veto-player policy risk; Hicken explains Thailand's post-1997 party transformation and Thailand-Philippines recovery; Wade adds the global financial system caveat; Malesky, Abrami, and Zheng explain why two single-party regimes diverged on inequality.
