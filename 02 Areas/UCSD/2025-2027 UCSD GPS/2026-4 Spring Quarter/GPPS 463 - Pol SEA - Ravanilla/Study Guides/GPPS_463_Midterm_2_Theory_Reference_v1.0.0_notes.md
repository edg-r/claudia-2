## Exam Orientation

Midterm 2 moves from colonial legacies into postwar growth, crisis, and authoritarian institutional variation. The central question is not simply why some countries grew, crashed, or redistributed. It is why similar regional pressures produced different political-economic outcomes once filtered through domestic institutions, elite coalitions, state capacity, party systems, and exposure to global finance.

Use these pages as mechanism cards. For short essays, do not merely identify the reading. State the causal chain:

**condition -> institution or coalition -> policy choice -> economic or political outcome -> limits**

The most reusable comparison set is:

- **Thailand:** high growth through entrepreneur-led, market-facing accumulation; severe AFC shock; crisis produced constitutional reform, Thai Rak Thai, and more decisive but more centralized politics.
- **Singapore:** state-built comparative advantage; markets used inside strong PAP coordination; growth through labor discipline, forced savings, infrastructure, MNE attraction, and phase-by-phase upgrading.
- **Philippines:** weaker growth and weaker reform momentum; mild AFC shock reduced pressure for institutional overhaul; post-crisis political instability kept fiscal, GOCC, and revenue problems alive.
- **Vietnam:** single-party regime with broader internal representation; more equalizing transfers and lower inequality than China despite similar authoritarian classification.

## 1. Growth Accounting and the "Myth" of the Asian Miracle

**Main source:** Krugman (1994)
**Lecture emphasis:** LD8 and LD9 use Cobb-Douglas/growth accounting to distinguish input growth from efficiency growth.

### Situation

Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia grew at 7-8 percent during the Asian Miracle years, but Krugman warns that high growth can come from mobilizing more labor and capital rather than from becoming more productive.

### Core Intuition

Krugman's key move is to demystify rapid growth. Output can rise because an economy uses more **inputs** such as capital, labor, education, land, savings, and work hours, or because it raises **total factor productivity** by using those inputs more efficiently. If growth is mainly input-driven, it can be spectacular for a while but eventually slows because labor-force participation, schooling, and savings rates cannot rise forever. This is why Krugman compares East Asian growth to Soviet growth: both looked miraculous from the outside, but growth accounting made them look less mysterious and less infinitely sustainable.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Growth accounting:** Decomposes output growth into growth from inputs and growth from productivity.
- **Cobb-Douglas production function:** The lecture shorthand is `Y = A x K x L`, where output depends on productivity, capital, and labor.
- **Total factor productivity (TFP):** The efficiency residual. It captures better technology, organization, management, and input use.
- **Input mobilization:** Growth through more labor, more education, more capital, higher savings, longer hours, and higher female or rural-to-urban participation.
- **Diminishing returns:** The reason input-driven growth slows. A first machine, school, or worker may add a lot; the next one adds less.
- **Perspiration rather than inspiration:** Krugman's image for growth driven by effort and sacrifice rather than technological leap.
- **Deferred gratification:** Krugman's practical "secret" of Asian growth: high savings and willingness to sacrifice current consumption.

### Assumptions

- Inputs can be measured well enough to separate input accumulation from productivity growth.
- Sustained per-capita growth ultimately requires rising output per unit of input.
- High savings, education gains, and labor-force increases face natural ceilings.
- Recent growth rates should not be extrapolated without checking the source of growth.

### Strengths

- Excellent for puncturing vague "Asian values" or authoritarian-superiority explanations.
- Gives a simple exam vocabulary for comparing Thailand and Singapore: both mobilized inputs, but the state and coalition mechanisms differed.
- Explains why an economy can grow quickly and still be vulnerable to slowdown.
- Helps link LD8 Thailand and LD9 Singapore to the same production-function framework.

### Weaknesses

- It can understate institutional variation by treating the key question as accounting rather than politics.
- It risks flattening Singapore's policy sophistication into mere input mobilization.
- It is less useful for explaining distribution, crisis response, or party-system change.
- It is skeptical about the miracle but does not fully explain why some Southeast Asian states mobilized resources better than others.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use Krugman when the prompt asks whether the Asian Miracle was really a miracle, whether growth was sustainable, or how to compare Thailand/Singapore with the rest of the region. The safe phrasing is: "Krugman does not deny growth; he denies that growth alone proves superior institutions or limitless productivity catch-up."

## 2. Thailand's Market-Oriented, Entrepreneur-Led Miracle

**Lecture source:** Lecture Day 8 slides
**Lecture emphasis:** Thailand grew through Chinese entrepreneurial networks, open trade, macro stability, land/labor inputs, and a small state rather than Korea/Taiwan/Singapore-style industrial planning.

### Situation

Thailand experienced rapid postwar growth despite never building a classic developmental state, which makes it the main Southeast Asian case for a more market-oriented miracle.

### Core Intuition

Thailand's growth story complicates the idea that East Asian success requires heavy state direction. The Thai state stayed relatively small, preserved macroeconomic stability, opened the economy, and left much of the economic sector to business entrepreneurs, especially Chinese-Thai commercial networks. Growth drew on factor accumulation: capital inflows, domestic savings, land expansion, female labor-force participation, rural labor, and export production. The state mattered, but more as a provider of stability and a permissive environment than as a planner selecting national champions.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Non-developmental state:** A state that supports growth without the intensive industrial policy associated with Korea, Taiwan, or Singapore.
- **Chinese-Thai entrepreneurs:** Commercial networks in trade, finance, rice exports, supply chains, and investment.
- **Bowring Treaty legacy:** Nineteenth-century opening that constrained tariff autonomy and pushed Siam toward trade openness.
- **Macroeconomic orthodoxy:** Price stability, manageable deficits, credible exchange-rate and fiscal policy, and external balance.
- **Small government:** LD8 highlights limited state size and market-oriented development.
- **Factor accumulation:** Land, capital, labor, education, and FDI inputs that drove growth.
- **TFP problem:** Input growth alone faces diminishing returns, so Thailand also needed productivity gains through better networks, investment conditions, and structural transformation.

### Assumptions

- Private entrepreneurs can coordinate investment when the state keeps macro conditions stable.
- Openness and FDI can raise growth without strong industrial planning.
- Chinese business networks can substitute partly for formal state coordination.
- Laborers and rural groups can be politically managed or excluded without immediately derailing growth.

### Strengths

- Strong contrast case to Singapore's active state and Vietnam's single-party redistribution.
- Explains why Thailand could grow despite weak programmatic parties and fragmented pre-1997 politics.
- Connects growth to social structure: business networks mattered, not just policy settings.
- Keeps colonial history in view: Thailand avoided formal colonization but accepted unequal treaties and external economic pressure.

### Weaknesses

- Growth created inequality and urban-rural divides that later fed political conflict.
- Weak or fragmented political institutions limited national public-goods provision.
- Financial-sector vulnerabilities and speculative capital inflows helped set up the 1997 crisis.
- Exclusion of labor and rural constituencies made the growth model politically brittle.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use Thailand as the "market-oriented but not laissez-faire" Southeast Asian miracle. It fits prompts about why only some countries experienced the Asian Miracle, whether developmental states are necessary, and why pre-crisis growth could coexist with post-crisis vulnerability.

## 3. Singapore's State-Created Comparative Advantage

**Lecture source:** Lecture Day 9 slides
**Lecture emphasis:** Singapore was not laissez-faire. The government used markets, but also deliberately distorted prices and built infrastructure to create comparative advantage.

### Situation

Singapore entered independence in 1965 as a small, resource-poor, vulnerable city-state with high unemployment, ethnic tension, weak military capacity, and dependence on entrepot trade.

### Core Intuition

Singapore's model was a market-facing form of state capitalism. The PAP state did not abolish markets; it organized the conditions under which export capitalism could work. It controlled wages, disciplined labor, forced savings through the CPF, built housing and infrastructure, attracted multinational firms, and shifted the economy across phases: low-value export manufacturing, high-value manufacturing, services, and global business functions. The key is that Singapore generated comparative advantage through policy rather than discovering it passively.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **State-created comparative advantage:** Government action changes cost structures and capabilities so firms can compete internationally.
- **Export-oriented industrialization (EOI):** Producing for external markets rather than protecting domestic import-substitution industries.
- **Labor and wage control:** NTUC and the National Wages Council helped keep labor costs predictable and production attractive.
- **Central Provident Fund (CPF):** Compulsory savings that financed housing, infrastructure, and state capacity.
- **Economic Development Board (EDB):** State agency for attracting FDI through tax incentives, industrial estates, and investor support.
- **MNE-led development:** Foreign firms substituted for missing domestic industrial entrepreneurs.
- **Jurong Industrial Estate:** Example of infrastructure as active industrial policy.
- **Phase strategy:** Singapore repeatedly upgraded from labor-intensive manufacturing to high-value manufacturing to business services.
- **External economy / SIJORI Growth Triangle:** Singapore extended its production platform beyond the island by linking capital, logistics, and management to lower-cost neighboring space in Johor and Riau.
- **Administrative state:** Depoliticized, capable bureaucracy used to steer economic transformation.

### Assumptions

- A capable and disciplined state can alter incentives without destroying market signals.
- Foreign capital can drive national development if the state controls the local platform.
- Labor discipline and social policy can produce stability at the cost of political contestation.
- Small size and strategic location make some interventions easier than in larger states.

### Strengths

- Best case for active industrial policy in the Midterm 2 scope.
- Shows why "openness" and "state intervention" are not opposites.
- Explains how a resource-poor country can manufacture advantages through policy sequencing.
- Links growth to nation-building: HDB integration, bilingual policy, multiracialism, and national education helped build cohesion.

### Weaknesses

- Hard to replicate because Singapore is a city-state with unusual location, PAP dominance, port/logistics advantages, and high foreign-firm presence.
- Heavy labor discipline and political control limit the model's democratic appeal.
- Dependence on MNEs and global/regional demand makes Singapore vulnerable to external shocks.
- Krugman's critique still applies: some growth came from input mobilization, especially savings, education, and labor shifts.
- Regional production networks can reduce domestic constraints but also deepen exposure to external demand and neighborhood instability.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use Singapore when the prompt asks about the role of the state, how countries created growth conditions, or whether the Asian Miracle vindicates markets or industrial policy. The answer is both: Singapore promoted trade openness while deliberately shaping wages, savings, infrastructure, and investment.

## 4. Wade's "Wheels Within Wheels" Critique of the Asian Crisis

**Main source:** Wade (2000)
**Lecture emphasis:** LD10 gives the basic AFC intuition, including hot money, weak regulation, hidden nonperforming loans, dollar pegs, and capital flight. Wade pushes the explanation outward to global finance.

### Situation

The Asian Financial Crisis began in Thailand in 1997 and spread through Malaysia, the Philippines, Indonesia, Korea, Hong Kong, and beyond, despite many countries having previously strong growth records.

### Core Intuition

Wade rejects the idea that the crisis was mainly proof that the Asian model had failed. He argues that the crisis emerged from the interaction between domestic vulnerabilities and the outer machinery of international capital markets. Post-Bretton Woods dollar finance, Japanese and European recession, large capital inflows, reserve accumulation, credit booms, asset inflation, and sudden reversals created the crash environment. Domestic governance mattered, but it was not the whole story. The metaphor is **wheels within wheels**: national and regional factors were inner wheels, while global liquidity, capital flows, and core-economy policies were outer wheels that made the crisis possible.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Failure of Asian governance theory:** The official-academic view that cronyism, weak transparency, weak prudential regulation, fixed exchange rates, and moral hazard caused the crisis.
- **Failure of international financial markets theory:** Wade's alternative, emphasizing volatile cross-border capital, core-economy policy, and global financial architecture.
- **Hot money:** Short-term speculative capital flows that can enter and exit rapidly.
- **Open capital account:** A policy environment where cross-border financial flows move freely, increasing exposure to sudden stops.
- **Credit boom:** Rapid lending expansion fueled by inflows and reserve accumulation.
- **Moral hazard:** The claim that investors lent carelessly because they expected bailouts.
- **Capital controls:** Wade's preferred policy tool for reducing exposure to destabilizing inflows and outflows.
- **Washington Consensus:** The policy package of liberalization, transparency, contract enforcement, capital-market finance, and reduced state intervention that crisis narratives often promoted.

### Assumptions

- Emerging markets are structurally vulnerable when exposed to large, reversible capital flows.
- Core economies and international institutions shape crisis risk, not only domestic borrowers and regulators.
- Financial liberalization can create instability even when macro fundamentals look decent.
- Policy prescriptions after a crisis are politically loaded; they can serve creditor and core-economy interests.

### Strengths

- Corrects overly domestic-blame explanations of the AFC.
- Explains why crisis spread regionally and why capital-flow reversal was so damaging.
- Connects Southeast Asia to global political economy, especially the United States, Japan, IMF, and investor behavior.
- Gives a strong counterpoint to MacIntyre's domestic-institutional framework.

### Weaknesses

- Less precise than MacIntyre for explaining variation across Thailand, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Indonesia.
- Can understate genuine domestic weaknesses such as crony lending, weak regulation, dollar pegs, and nonperforming loans.
- Policy solutions like capital controls are politically and administratively difficult.
- It explains crisis vulnerability better than post-crisis institutional reform.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Pair Wade with MacIntyre. Wade answers "why did the region become vulnerable to crisis?" MacIntyre answers "why did country responses differ?" A high-scoring essay can say that Wade supplies the global shock structure while MacIntyre explains domestic filtering through veto authority.

## 5. MacIntyre's Veto-Player Model of Crisis Response

**Main source:** MacIntyre (2001)
**Lecture emphasis:** LD10 explicitly uses institutional analysis and veto players/points to explain why Thailand and the Philippines differed in crisis response and recovery.

### Situation

Investors facing the Asian Financial Crisis needed governments to respond credibly and decisively, but different political systems created different levels of policy risk.

### Core Intuition

MacIntyre argues that the distribution of **veto authority** shapes policy posture, and policy posture shapes investor confidence. Too few veto players create **policy volatility**, because a powerful executive can change policy abruptly and investors doubt commitments. Too many veto players create **policy rigidity**, because governments cannot move quickly from the status quo. The best investment climate lies in the middle: enough veto players to make policy credible, but not so many that action becomes impossible. This produces a U-shaped relationship between veto-player concentration and policy risk.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Veto player:** An actor or group whose agreement is required to change policy.
- **Veto point:** An institutional location where policy can be blocked.
- **Credible commitment:** Investors believe government will stick to announced policy.
- **Decisiveness:** Government can act quickly and coherently when conditions change.
- **Policy rigidity:** Too many veto players slow or block needed adjustment.
- **Policy volatility:** Too few veto players allow sudden and unpredictable swings.
- **Policy risk:** The investment danger created by either rigidity or volatility.
- **U-shaped risk curve:** Investor risk is high at both extremes of veto-player distribution and lower in intermediate configurations.

### Assumptions

- Investors care about government policy responses during crisis, not just pre-crisis fundamentals.
- Political institutions shape what policies are possible and how credible they look.
- Veto authority can be compared across democratic, semi-democratic, and authoritarian systems.
- Crisis conditions make both credibility and decisiveness especially valuable.

### Strengths

- Gives a clean exam framework for comparing Southeast Asian AFC outcomes.
- Explains why institutional extremes can both be bad, though for opposite reasons.
- Bridges political science and investor behavior.
- Fits LD10's question about why domestic political environments affected recovery.

### Weaknesses

- Formal veto players do not capture every informal actor, such as militaries, business groups, monarchies, courts, street movements, or royalist networks.
- It can over-simplify country cases by reducing them to veto counts.
- It explains investment response better than democratic quality or distributive outcomes.
- The "middle" is analytically attractive but hard to identify precisely in messy politics.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use MacIntyre for any question about AFC recovery, institutional response, or investor confidence. The clean sentence: "MacIntyre's mechanism is not that democracy is good or authoritarianism is bad; it is that the distribution of veto authority changes the tradeoff between credible commitment and decisive adjustment."

## 6. Hicken 2008: Crisis Severity, Reform Momentum, and the Thailand-Philippines Contrast

**Main source:** Hicken (2008)
**Lecture emphasis:** LD10 contrasts Thailand's collapse and recovery with the Philippines' mild shock and post-crisis sluggishness.

### Situation

Thailand was hit harder by the AFC, but the crisis generated reform momentum; the Philippines was hit less severely, but the milder shock reduced pressure to fix deeper political and economic vulnerabilities.

### Core Intuition

Hicken's paradox is that a severe crisis can be politically useful because it exposes institutional failure and creates pressure for reform. Thailand's collapse did not just damage the economy; it discredited existing parties, elevated constitutional reform, pushed business into direct politics, and eventually produced a more decisive post-1997 party system. The Philippines, by contrast, avoided a full political crisis. The Ramos administration's response initially looked successful, so elites lacked urgency for deeper reform. Post-crisis politics then slid into instability and malaise under Estrada and Arroyo, leaving debt, revenue, GOCC, and governance problems unresolved.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Crisis as catalyst:** Severe economic shock can make previously blocked reforms politically possible.
- **Mild-shock trap:** A less severe crisis can validate the status quo and preserve weak institutions.
- **Thailand as ground zero:** The baht collapse and severe contraction exposed the vulnerabilities of Thailand's boom.
- **Philippines as "sick man" problem:** The country avoided the worst initial damage but continued lagging behind neighbors.
- **Ramos reforms:** Liberalizing reforms before the crisis helped cushion the Philippines but did not create lasting reform momentum.
- **Constitutional reform as credibility signal:** In Thailand, support for the 1997 constitution became tied to investor and voter confidence.
- **Political malaise:** Low reform capacity caused by legitimacy crises, scandals, impeachment struggles, and executive survival politics.
- **Unaddressed vulnerabilities:** Philippine public debt, weak tax/revenue effort, and poorly performing GOCCs.

### Assumptions

- Reform requires both institutional tools and political urgency.
- Severe crisis can weaken entrenched interests that would normally block reform.
- Mild crisis does not necessarily mean better long-run prospects.
- Presidents and party leaders must have both incentives and capacity to build reform coalitions.

### Strengths

- Preserves the counterintuitive mechanism Edgar flagged in Poseidon's feedback: a milder crisis can be worse if it lets weak institutions survive.
- Explains why Thailand's painful collapse could produce more reform than the Philippines' smaller downturn.
- Connects AFC outcomes to party-system and constitutional change.
- Gives concrete cases for crisis-generated political economy.

### Weaknesses

- Severe crisis is not automatically reformist; it can also produce instability, backlash, coups, or authoritarian concentration.
- Thailand's later Thaksin-era concentration and 2006 coup show reform can create new vulnerabilities.
- The Philippines' weak reform record also reflects deeper structural and historical problems, not just mild crisis severity.
- Hicken's Thailand optimism is tempered by the later instability he himself notes.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use this reading when the prompt asks why Thailand recovered faster, why the Philippines remained sluggish, or whether crisis can help reform. The key exam line: "Hicken's argument is not that Thailand suffered less; it is that suffering more created the political pressure to change institutions."

## 7. Hicken 2006: Party Fabrication and the Rise of Thai Rak Thai

**Main source:** Hicken (2006)
**Lecture emphasis:** LD10 highlights post-1997 constitutional and electoral reforms, Thai Rak Thai, and Thaksin as a decisive veto player.

### Situation

After the 1997 crisis and constitution, Thai Rak Thai won a strong mandate in 2001 and replaced Thailand's short-lived multiparty coalition pattern with a more centralized party government.

### Core Intuition

Hicken argues that Thaksin's wealth and the crisis mattered, but they are not enough to explain Thai Rak Thai's rise. The 1997 constitution changed the incentives of Thai politics. It reduced party fragmentation, strengthened party leaders, discouraged party switching, introduced single-member districts and a national party list, and increased the prime minister's leverage over coalition partners and intraparty factions. Thai Rak Thai took advantage of this new institutional landscape to build a national party, centralize policymaking, and deliver programmatic policies. The result was greater decisiveness, but also the "Thaksinization" of politics and erosion of checks.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Party fabrication:** Institutionally enabled construction of a stronger party system, not just organic party development.
- **1997 constitution:** Thailand's reform constitution adopted during the AFC.
- **Mixed-member system:** 400 single-seat constituency seats plus 100 national party-list seats.
- **National party list:** Encouraged parties to make broader national appeals.
- **90-day membership requirement:** Reduced last-minute party switching by requiring candidates to belong to a party before elections.
- **Fragmentation reduction:** Reforms lowered the number of parties and improved the chances of majority government.
- **Prime ministerial leverage:** Cabinet rules and party discipline strengthened the PM relative to factions and coalition partners.
- **Superintendent institutions:** Electoral Commission, Constitutional Court, anti-corruption commission, and human rights institutions meant to check power.
- **Thaksinization:** Centralization of political and economic authority around Thaksin and Thai Rak Thai.

### Assumptions

- Electoral institutions shape party incentives and coordination.
- Stronger parties can improve policy decisiveness and national programmatic appeals.
- Reducing fragmentation can improve governance, but only if checks remain credible.
- Politicians adapt strategically to new constitutional rules.

### Strengths

- Explains why Thai politics changed so sharply after 1997.
- Links institutional design to party-system outcomes and policy capacity.
- Makes sense of LD10's claim that Thai Rak Thai became a decisive actor.
- Shows the tradeoff between fragmented coalition politics and centralized majority politics.

### Weaknesses

- Institutional reform was necessary but not sufficient; Thaksin's wealth, crisis context, and weakened opponents mattered.
- Reformers did not anticipate one-party dominance or weakened oversight institutions.
- Stronger party government produced decisiveness but also abuse-of-power risks.
- Informal veto players later returned through protests, courts, military intervention, and royalist networks.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use Hicken 2006 to explain how Thailand moved from too many veto players and weak parties toward a more decisive party system. Then use Hicken 2008 to explain why the crisis created the conditions for that reform to matter.

## 8. Malesky, Abrami, and Zheng: Inequality Inside Single-Party Regimes

**Main source:** Malesky, Abrami, and Zheng (2011)
**Lecture emphasis:** LD11 asks why Vietnam's inequality remained moderate relative to China's despite both being single-party regimes.

### Situation

China and Vietnam both grew rapidly under communist single-party regimes, but China became much more unequal while Vietnam maintained more moderate inequality.

### Core Intuition

The authors open the black box of "single-party regime." Standard regime-type labels code China and Vietnam the same, but their internal elite institutions differ. China concentrates key economic decisions in a smaller Politburo and Politburo Standing Committee. Vietnam's Central Committee is larger, more diverse, more electorally competitive, and more representative of provinces and mass organizations. Because Vietnamese leaders must maintain a broader internal coalition, they spend more on equalizing transfers, poverty alleviation, education, health, and development investment across provinces. The result is lower inequality, even though standard economic and sociocultural explanations predict Vietnam should be more unequal.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Authoritarian institutional variation:** Differences inside regime types that standard democracy/autocracy measures miss.
- **Central Committee (CCOM):** Vietnam's key 160-member deliberative body, broader and more diverse than China's top bodies.
- **Politburo / PBSC:** China's narrower elite decision-making institutions.
- **Winning coalition:** The set of actors whose support leaders need to stay in power or pass major policy.
- **Coalition breadth:** The more actors and preferences included, the more policy must account for redistribution and public goods.
- **Equalizing transfers:** Revenue transfers from rich to poor provinces for education, infrastructure, welfare, and poverty reduction.
- **Semicompetitive internal elections:** Internal party selection rules that force leaders to consider more delegates and interests.
- **Diffused versus concentrated authority:** Vietnam diffused authority after late-1980s shocks; China concentrated it after Tiananmen.
- **Fiscal equalization:** Budget architecture designed to reduce regional inequality.

### Assumptions

- Even authoritarian parties need internal coalitions.
- Broader and more competitive elite institutions increase redistributive pressure.
- Public expenditure choices are political choices, not automatic products of growth.
- Party institutions can shape distributive outcomes without democratization.

### Strengths

- Directly answers LD11's puzzle about Vietnam versus China.
- Prevents lazy "both are communist, so they should behave the same" reasoning.
- Gives concrete measures: Vietnam's equalizing transfers were roughly 6 percent of GDP versus China's 2 percent, and Vietnam spent more on poverty alleviation and basic education.
- Links authoritarian institutions to welfare and inequality, not only regime survival.

### Weaknesses

- Redistribution may come at a growth cost if Vietnam spreads public investment too widely.
- The framework focuses on elite institutions more than citizen participation or repression.
- Broader internal coalitions do not equal democracy.
- The paper's comparison is strongest for inequality, less complete for explaining corruption, growth quality, or future regime durability.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Use this framework whenever the prompt asks how Vietnam is doing under communism or why Vietnam and China differ. The strongest answer rejects usual suspects first: Kuznets, globalization, population, ethnicity, and macro regime type all fail; internal party institutions explain the divergence.

## 9. Lecture Framework: Vietnam's "Doing Well" Under Communism

**Main sources:** LD11 slides and Malesky et al. (2011)
**Lecture emphasis:** "Doing well" means high growth, low inequality, regime longevity, and quality of governance.

### Situation

Vietnam is a single-party regime, but the lecture frames it as doing comparatively well because it combines rapid growth with lower inequality than China.

### Core Intuition

LD11 uses Vietnam to show that authoritarian regime type is too blunt. The real question is how internal hierarchies distribute power. Vietnam's party institutions require leaders to build broader coalitions across central officials, SOEs, provincial leaders, mass organizations, business associations, education institutions, and other sectors. That broad coalition gives poorer and provincial interests more leverage than they have in China's narrower Politburo-centered system. The policy result is not just abstract equality talk; it appears in more equalizing transfers, poverty programs, basic education spending, and public-service redistribution.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Kuznets hypothesis:** Early growth from a low base should raise inequality before later equalization; LD11 notes this predicts Vietnam should be more unequal, but it is not.
- **Global integration explanation:** Openness can raise inequality, but Vietnam is more exposed to exports and FDI than China, so this explanation also fails.
- **Socio-cultural explanations:** Population size and ethnic heterogeneity do not explain the pattern; Vietnam has greater ethnic fractionalization than China in the slides.
- **Macro-level institutional sameness:** Both countries are single-party regimes, so regime type cannot do the work alone.
- **Public-service delivery:** The concrete site where institutional differences become visible.
- **Party congress incentives:** Vietnamese public investment rises before party congresses, suggesting leaders use redistribution to maintain internal support.

### Assumptions

- Inequality is politically mediated through fiscal choices.
- Provincial and mass-organization representation changes national spending incentives.
- Internal party competition can create accountability even without multiparty democracy.
- Good performance in Vietnam is relative, not a claim that repression or one-party rule is normatively ideal.

### Strengths

- Excellent for exam questions asking "what does it mean to do well?"
- Moves beyond growth-only metrics by including equity, governance, and regime durability.
- Lets Edgar compare Vietnam with Singapore and Thailand: all grew, but the institutional mechanisms differ sharply.
- Keeps the analysis inside Ravanilla's political-economy lens.

### Weaknesses

- Does not make Vietnam democratic or rights-protecting.
- Redistribution can produce inefficient project spreading.
- The model could change if Vietnamese party institutions centralize further.
- "Doing well" depends on which values are weighted: growth, equality, freedom, accountability, or stability.

### Exam Deployment Notes

For Vietnam, lead with the puzzle. Then quickly eliminate the usual suspects. Then explain internal party architecture: broader CCOM, competitive internal selection, provincial representation, equalizing transfers, lower Gini growth.

## 10. Cross-Reading Synthesis: The Midterm 2 Master Framework

### Situation

The same region faced postwar development, global capital exposure, and authoritarian or semi-democratic institutional constraints, but outcomes diverged across Thailand, Singapore, the Philippines, and Vietnam.

### Core Intuition

Midterm 2 is about domestic political institutions under regional and global pressure. Krugman gives the growth-accounting baseline: rapid growth is not automatically productivity miracle. Lecture Day 8 and Lecture Day 9 explain two Southeast Asian growth pathways: Thailand through entrepreneurs, openness, and a limited state; Singapore through an interventionist state that created market advantages. Wade and MacIntyre explain the AFC from different levels: global finance created systemic vulnerability, while domestic veto-player structures shaped crisis response. Hicken then shows how crisis severity can reshape institutions, especially Thailand's 1997 constitution and Thai Rak Thai. Malesky et al. move the institutional lens into authoritarian regimes, showing that internal party architecture shapes redistribution and inequality.

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Level of analysis:** Global finance, national institutions, party systems, elite coalitions, and local distribution.
- **Input versus productivity growth:** The growth baseline for the Asian Miracle.
- **State role spectrum:** Thailand's small, permissive state; Singapore's active administrative state; Vietnam's redistributive single-party state.
- **Crisis filtering:** External shocks do not determine outcomes directly; domestic institutions filter them.
- **Veto tradeoff:** Credibility and decisiveness must be balanced.
- **Reform momentum:** Severe crisis can weaken blockers and create constitutional or party-system change.
- **Authoritarian black box:** Single-party regimes differ internally in coalition breadth and accountability.

### Assumptions

- Institutions matter because they structure incentives, not because they mechanically determine outcomes.
- Growth, crisis response, and redistribution require different institutional capacities.
- Domestic and international explanations should be layered rather than treated as mutually exclusive.
- Exam answers should use cases to test mechanisms, not just as illustrations.

### Strengths

- Lets one essay connect all of LD8-LD11.
- Keeps Ravanilla's institutions, political economy, and comparative development lens visible.
- Gives clean contrast pairs: Thailand/Singapore, Thailand/Philippines, Vietnam/China, Wade/MacIntyre, Krugman/LD9 Singapore.
- Helps avoid one-note explanations such as "culture," "authoritarianism," "globalization," or "markets."

### Weaknesses

- Synthesis can become too broad if it does not name specific mechanisms.
- The readings disagree about emphasis, especially Wade's global finance account versus domestic-institution accounts.
- Some theories explain growth, some crisis response, and some inequality; do not force one framework to answer every question.
- Case timing matters: pre-1997 Thailand, post-1997 Thailand, and Thaksin-era Thailand are analytically different.

### Exam Deployment Notes

Default essay skeleton:

1. State the outcome: growth, crisis recovery, reform, or inequality.
2. Name the key mechanism from the relevant reading.
3. Give one concrete case detail.
4. Add a contrast case.
5. State the limitation.

Example: "Thailand's AFC recovery cannot be explained only by the size of the shock.
