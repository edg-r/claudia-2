# GPPS 463 LD18 One-Pager: Ethnic Diversity, Segregation, and Public Goods

## Reading

Tajima, Y., Samphantharak, K., & Ostwald, K. (2018). Ethnic segregation and public goods: Evidence from Indonesia. *American Political Science Review, 112*(3), 637-653.

## Core Question

Is ethnic and racial diversity itself bad for public goods provision in Southeast Asia, or does the effect depend on how groups are spatially organized and how local governments allocate resources?

## Main Argument

The article complicates the common "diversity penalty" argument. Prior political economy work often finds that ethnically diverse areas provide fewer public goods because diversity makes collective action harder. Tajima, Samphantharak, and Ostwald agree that ethnic fractionalization can hurt public goods provision, but they argue that **spatial segregation** changes the story. When ethnic groups are distributed into relatively homogeneous villages within a diverse district, those villages may organize more effectively and lobby district officials for resources. Public goods given to one village then become leverage for other villages to demand matching investments, creating what the authors call **spatial interdependence**.

The key move is to separate the **decision level** from the **user level**. District officials often decide where public goods go, but villages are where people actually use schools, clinics, roads, and other goods. A district can be diverse overall while its villages are either integrated or segregated. The authors argue that this lower-level spatial pattern matters because homogeneous villages can advocate more coherently than mixed villages.

## Mechanism

The article identifies two linked problems:

- **Consensus problem:** Diverse communities may have more difficulty agreeing on which public goods they want or how to support them.
- **Advocacy problem:** Even if they have needs, diverse or integrated communities may be less effective at lobbying decision-makers.

Segregated villages can overcome these problems more easily because local ethnic homogeneity can make coordination simpler. Once one village gets a school, road, or clinic, other villages can use that visible disparity as evidence that they deserve similar investment. The result is a "sibling rivalry" dynamic where public goods in one locality can raise demands in neighboring localities.

## Evidence

The authors test the argument using highly disaggregated Indonesian data from around 2000:

- Individual-level population census data for ethnic fractionalization and segregation.
- Village census data covering more than 50,000 villages.
- Public goods outcomes including middle schools, high schools, health centers, and asphalt roads.

They find that ethnic segregation is positively associated with public goods provision for goods where district officials have discretion over placement. Segregated areas tend to have better access to middle schools, high schools, health centers, and asphalt roads even after controlling for ethnic fractionalization at both the district and village levels.

The argument is stronger because they also test goods where local advocacy should matter less. Primary schools and health subcenters were more heavily shaped by national formula-based programs, so local lobbying should not drive placement as much. For those goods, ethnic segregation is not significantly associated with provision. That contrast supports the mechanism: segregation matters when local advocacy and district discretion matter.

## Why Indonesia Matters

Indonesia is a useful case because it is large, ethnically diverse, and administratively decentralized enough for local allocation politics to matter. The article focuses on the late New Order period, before Indonesia's major post-Suharto decentralization reforms, but the authors argue the mechanism may become even stronger under democratization and decentralization because local advocacy becomes more important.

This matters for Southeast Asia because many states in the region are highly diverse, unevenly decentralized, and still wrestling with how to distribute public goods across ethnic, religious, and regional communities.

## Big Takeaway

The reading does **not** say ethnic diversity is simply good or bad. It says the effect of diversity depends on political geography and institutional design. Diverse districts with segregated villages may get more public goods because homogeneous local communities can advocate more effectively, while diverse and integrated communities may be disadvantaged in decentralized allocation systems.

## Tension / Critique

The finding creates an uncomfortable policy tradeoff. Segregation may improve local advocacy for public goods, but it can also reduce interethnic contact, reinforce group boundaries, and weaken shared national identity. The authors therefore do not celebrate segregation; instead, they warn that decentralized systems may unintentionally reward segregated communities and penalize integrated ones unless higher levels of government compensate for that imbalance.

## Class Discussion Hook

If integration is normatively attractive because it can reduce prejudice and build national identity, but segregation may help communities secure public goods, how should Southeast Asian governments design decentralization and redistribution policies?

## Terms To Remember

- **Ethnic fractionalization:** The probability that two randomly selected people in a unit belong to different ethnic groups.
- **Spatial segregation:** The degree to which ethnic groups are separated into different local communities within a larger unit.
- **Decision level:** The administrative level where resource allocation decisions are made, such as the district.
- **User level:** The local level where public goods are actually consumed, such as the village.
- **Spatial interdependence:** The idea that public goods provision in one community affects other communities' ability to demand similar goods.
