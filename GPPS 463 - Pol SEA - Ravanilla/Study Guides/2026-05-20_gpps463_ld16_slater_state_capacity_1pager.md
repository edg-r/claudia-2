# GPPS 463 LD16 One-Pager: Slater on State Capacity

**Class:** Wednesday, May 20, 2026  
**Topic:** Why do some countries have high state capacity but not others?  
**Reading:** Slater (2010), Ch. 1, "To Extract and To Organize"

## Core Answer

Slater's answer is coalitional. High state capacity does not appear just because rulers want it, because outsiders pressure them, or because a country has the right colonial inheritance. It emerges when elites have a shared reason to support stronger central authority. In Southeast Asia, Slater argues that especially threatening forms of contentious politics made some elites coordinate around **protection pacts**, broad coalitions that backed stronger states and tighter authoritarian controls as protection from mass unrest (Slater, 2010, pp. 5-6).

The punchline for class: **state capacity is built when elites decide that a stronger state protects them better than a weaker one.** Without that shared threat, elites are more likely to free ride, factionalize, or bargain for private benefits instead of supporting durable institutions.

## Mechanism Chain

**Contentious politics creates threat.** Slater defines contentious politics broadly: strikes, riots, rebellions, student protest, terrorism, barricades, revolutions, and separatist insurgencies. The key is not unrest in general, but unrest that powerful elites see as endemic, unmanageable, and dangerous to their property, status, or safety.

**Threat creates elite collective action.** When elites face a shared threat, they can overcome their own collective-action problem. They may not trust each other, but they can still support a common institutional response: stronger coercion, stronger extraction, stronger ruling parties, and more disciplined militaries.

**Elite collective action builds institutions.** Slater links four puzzles to one coalitional puzzle: state capacity, party strength, military cohesion, and authoritarian durability all depend on whether elites act collectively. The local PDF states the question directly: why are elites more prone to act collectively in some systems than others? (Slater, 2010, p. 4).

**Protection pacts let regimes extract and organize.** With a protection pact, rulers can extract resources from elites, especially taxes, and organize elite support into durable institutions. This is why the chapter title matters: the state becomes strong when it can both **extract** resources and **organize** power.

## Southeast Asia Cases to Keep Straight

- **Malaysia and Singapore: domination.** Slater classifies both as high state capacity, high party strength, high military cohesion, and high authoritarian durability. Their protection-pact pathway came from intense class conflict with urban and communal implications, which made elites support strong ruling institutions (Slater, 2010, pp. 8, 24-25).
- **Philippines, South Vietnam, and Thailand: fragmentation.** These cases lacked the same broad protection pact. Rulers relied more on provision pacts, distributing benefits to elites, but this produced weaker states, weaker parties, weaker militaries, and fragile regimes (Slater, 2010, pp. 24-25).
- **Burma and Indonesia: militarization or intermediate order.** Regional rebellion can unify military elites without creating a broad civilian elite coalition. Burma gets military cohesion without strong civilian institutions. Indonesia combines regional and class conflict, producing an intermediate pathway under Suharto (Slater, 2010, pp. 25-27).

## Seminar-Ready Line

Slater is not saying authoritarianism automatically creates strong states. He is saying strong states come from elite coalitions forged by threatening mass politics. Authoritarianism matters only when it rests on a protection pact broad enough to let rulers extract resources and organize power.

## Terms to Know

- **Infrastructural power:** the state's capacity to implement political objectives across society.
- **Contentious politics:** nonroutine mass mobilization, including strikes, riots, rebellions, protests, and insurgencies.
- **Elite collective action:** coordinated behavior by powerful groups who support a shared institutional project.
- **Protection pact:** a broad elite coalition supporting stronger state power and tighter authoritarian controls against threatening mass unrest.
- **Provision pact:** a weaker arrangement where rulers buy elite support through benefits rather than shared protection.
- **Domination:** strong state, party, military, and authoritarian institutions, especially Malaysia and Singapore.
- **Fragmentation:** weak or factionalized institutions, especially the Philippines, South Vietnam, and Thailand.
- **Militarization:** cohesive coercive institutions without broad civilian elite support, especially Burma.
