## Lecture Files

### `Lecture Day 8.pdf`

- **Lecture title:** "Why did only some countries experience the Asian Miracle?"
- **Likely session topic:** LD8 / Week 4, Asian Miracle and Thailand.
- **Citation details discoverable:** Nico Ravanilla, UCSD GPS, Apr. 22, 2026; 55 beamer pages.
- **Core argument:** Southeast Asia entered independence with uneven colonial legacies, uneven nationalist consolidation, and Cold War pressures. The "Asian Miracle" happened only in selected economies because growth required both input mobilization and institutional/political conditions that supported savings, entrepreneurship, openness, and some productivity gains.
- **Named concepts/theories:** Asian Miracle, growth accounting, Cobb-Douglas production function, total factor productivity, input accumulation, diminishing marginal productivity, export-oriented growth, nationalism, Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, "point of no return" for colonial rule.
- **Cases/countries:** Thailand, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Philippines, Burma/Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, Brunei, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** factor accumulation, TFP, capital input, labor input, Bowring Treaty, extraterritoriality, Chinese merchant class, centralized bureaucracy, constitutional monarchy, military rule, rural-urban inequality, Hukbalahap/Huks, Viet Minh, Aung San, "Unity in Diversity."
- **Reading map:** Pairs most directly with Jansen (2001), already summarized elsewhere in the course folder; Krugman (1994) is useful as the growth-accounting counterpoint.

### `Lecture Day 9.pdf`

- **Lecture title:** "Asian Miracle: How Did Singapore Do It?"
- **Likely session topic:** LD9 / Week 5, Singapore model of development.
- **Citation details discoverable:** Nico Ravanilla, UCSD GPS, Apr. 27, 2026; 77 PDF pages / 39 slide-numbered pages.
- **Core argument:** Singapore's success came from an unusually capable, adaptive state that used market openness plus deliberate state intervention to shift the economy across development phases, from labor-intensive export manufacturing to high-value manufacturing, services, external regional production networks, and diversification after the Asian Financial Crisis.
- **Named concepts/theories:** export-oriented industrialization, active industrial policy, Washington Consensus contrast, developmental state, structural transformation, comparative advantage, forced savings, FDI attraction, external economy, SIJORI Growth Triangle, nation-building, multiracialism.
- **Cases/countries:** Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, Philippines, Hong Kong, South Korea, Taiwan, China.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** PAP, Lee Kuan Yew, Economic Development Board, Jurong Industrial Estate, CPF, POSB, HDB, NTUC, National Wages Council, DBS, Neptune Orient Lines, GIC, corrective wage policy, Economic Review Committee, integrated resorts, Ethnic Integration Policy, National Service, bilingual policy.
- **Reading map:** Pairs with Huff (1995) for Singapore's state-capitalist model and with Krugman (1994) as the skeptical claim that Singapore's growth was resource mobilization rather than true productivity miracle.

### `Lecture Day 10.pdf`

- **Lecture title:** "Why was the Asian Financial Crisis unequally felt in the region?"
- **Likely session topic:** LD10 / Week 5, Asian Financial Crisis and political institutions.
- **Citation details discoverable:** Nico Ravanilla, UCSD GPS, Apr. 29, 2026; 46 PDF pages / 31 slide-numbered pages.
- **Core argument:** The crisis had common regional financial triggers, but recovery varied because domestic political institutions shaped crisis response. Thailand was hit hard but eventually reformed; the Philippines was less badly hit but the mild crisis reduced reform pressure and allowed old vulnerabilities to persist.
- **Named concepts/theories:** veto players/veto points, credible commitment, decisiveness, policy rigidity, policy volatility, hot money, contagion, non-performing loans, crony capitalism, fixed exchange-rate peg, IMF conditionality, clientelism.
- **Cases/countries:** Thailand, Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, South Korea, Hong Kong.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** capital flight, foreign reserves, devaluation, speculative capital, balance of payments, IMF program, Thai Rak Thai, Thaksin Shinawatra, 1997 Constitution, list PR, multi-member plurality system, SMD/FPTP, pork barrel, GOCCs, anti-dynasty law.
- **Reading map:** Hicken (2008) is the required Thailand/Philippines comparison; MacIntyre (2001) supplies the veto-player model; Wade (2000) offers an external-capital-market explanation; Hicken (2006) deepens the Thai Rak Thai institutional mechanism.

### `Lecture Day 11.pdf`

- **Lecture title:** "How is Vietnam Doing Under Communism?"
- **Likely session topic:** LD11 / Week 6, Vietnam under communism and single-party regimes.
- **Citation details discoverable:** Nico Ravanilla, UCSD GPS, May 4, 2026; 60 PDF pages / 25 slide-numbered pages.
- **Core argument:** Vietnam and China are both fast-growing single-party regimes, but Vietnam kept inequality lower because its internal party institutions are broader and more competitive. ## Reading Files

### `krugman1994.pdf`

- **Title/citation details:** Paul Krugman, "The Myth of Asia's Miracle," *Foreign Affairs*, 73(6), 62-78, Nov.-Dec. 1994.
- **Likely session topic:** LD9 Asian Miracle/Singapore; optional theoretical foil for growth accounting.
- **Core argument:** East Asia's rapid growth was not a magical or permanently superior model. Much of it can be explained by extraordinary input mobilization, especially labor-force expansion, education, and physical capital investment; input-driven growth eventually faces diminishing returns.
- **Named concepts/theories:** growth accounting, input-driven growth, total factor productivity, diminishing returns, Soviet growth analogy, Asian tigers, deferred gratification.
- **Cases/countries:** Singapore, Soviet Union, East Asian newly industrialized economies, Japan, China, Hong Kong, South Korea, Taiwan.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** perspiration vs. inspiration, factor accumulation, efficiency growth, productivity convergence, capital deepening, labor-force participation.
- **Lecture tie:** LD9 uses Krugman to ask whether Singapore's "miracle" was actually a replicable state-capacity story or a one-time mobilization of capital, labor, and savings.

### `hicken2006.pdf`

- **Title/citation details:** Allen Hicken, "Party Fabrication: Constitutional Reform and the Rise of Thai Rak Thai," *Journal of East Asian Studies*, 6, 381-407, 2006.
- **Likely session topic:** LD10 background on Thailand's post-crisis institutional change.
- **Core argument:** Thaksin and Thai Rak Thai cannot be explained by Thaksin's personal wealth or the economic crisis alone. The 1997 Thai constitution changed electoral and party incentives by reducing party fragmentation and increasing prime-ministerial power relative to coalition partners and intraparty factions.
- **Named concepts/theories:** constitutional reform, electoral-system incentives, party fragmentation, majority-party government, personal vote, programmatic party-building, decentralization, superintendent institutions.
- **Cases/countries:** Thailand; comparisons invoked in the text include UMNO and PAP as dominant-party models.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** Thai Rak Thai, Thaksinization, 1997 Constitution, party switching, coalition government, CEO-governors, Constitutional Court, Election Commission, National Counter Corruption Commission.
- **Lecture tie:** Helps explain why LD10 treats Thai Rak Thai as a decisive post-crisis veto player and why fewer veto players can produce both decisive policy and democratic strain.

### `hicken2008.pdf`

- **Title/citation details:** Allen Hicken, "Politics of Economic Recovery in Thailand and the Philippines," forthcoming chapter in *East Asia Ten Years After the Crisis*, edited by Andrew MacIntyre, T. J. Pempel, and John Ravenhill, Cornell University Press.
- **Likely session topic:** LD10 required reading on Asian Financial Crisis recovery.
- **Core argument:** Thailand's deep crisis became a political crisis that opened space for institutional reform and business entry into party politics; the Philippines' milder crisis validated prior reforms and reduced urgency for deeper economic and political reform. The irony is that being hurt less in 1997 may have left the Philippines more vulnerable over time.
- **Named concepts/theories:** crisis-as-catalyst, political economy of recovery, institutional reform, reform momentum, governance breakdown, majority government, coalition-building.
- **Cases/countries:** Thailand, Philippines; references Malaysia and Indonesia as comparison points.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** Ramos administration, Estrada, Arroyo, GOCCs, debt burden, tax revenues, Thai Rak Thai, IMF, NPLs, Temasek/Shin Corp controversy, military coup.
- **Lecture tie:** LD10's Thailand-vs.-Philippines paradox is Hicken's argument in lecture form: crisis severity can create reform pressure, while a shallow crisis can postpone reform.

### `mcintyre2001.pdf`

- **Title/citation details:** Andrew MacIntyre, "Institutions and Investors: The Politics of the Economic Crisis in Southeast Asia," *International Organization*, 55(1), 81-122, Winter 2001.
- **Likely session topic:** LD10 optional framework reading.
- **Core argument:** Political institutions shaped investor reactions during the Asian Financial Crisis. The distribution of veto authority affects policy posture: too many veto players create policy rigidity, while too few create policy volatility. Both extremes raise policy risk for investors.
- **Named concepts/theories:** veto player framework, credible commitment, policy flexibility, policy rigidity, policy volatility, U-shaped policy risk, institutional framework -> policy posture -> investment.
- **Cases/countries:** Thailand, Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** veto authority, policy risk, investor confidence, investment reversal, one-veto-player system, coalition vetoes, formal vs. informal veto players, rigidity/volatility syndromes.
- **Lecture tie:** LD10 defines veto players almost directly from MacIntyre and uses the sweet spot between commitment and decisiveness to explain unequal crisis response.

### `wade2000.pdf`

- **Title/citation details:** Robert Wade, "Wheels Within Wheels: Rethinking the Asian Crisis and the Asian Model," *Annual Review of Political Science*, 3, 85-115, 2000.
- **Likely session topic:** LD10 optional counter-explanation for Asian Financial Crisis.
- **Core argument:** The crisis was caused less by the East Asian model itself than by international capital markets and policies of core economies, especially the United States and Japan. Post-Bretton Woods liquidity, capital inflows, credit booms, asset inflation, and industrial overcapacity made emerging markets vulnerable.
- **Named concepts/theories:** East Asian model, global financial liberalization, post-Bretton Woods system, capital controls, credit boom, asset inflation, overcapacity, international payments system, core-periphery financial vulnerability.
- **Cases/countries:** East Asia broadly, especially South Korea, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia; United States and Japan as core-economy drivers.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** capital controls, dollar reserves, foreign reserves, global finance, international clearing agency, surplus countries, core economies, "homegrown" crisis explanation.
- **Lecture tie:** Useful as a contrast to LD10's domestic-institutions argument: Wade says external capital structures matter more than domestic crony capitalism or "Asian model" failure.

### `malesky2011.pdf`

- **Title/citation details:** Edmund Malesky, Regina Abrami, and Yu Zheng, "Institutions and Inequality in Single-Party Regimes: A Comparative Analysis of Vietnam and China," *Comparative Politics*, 43(4), 401-419, July 2011.
- **Likely session topic:** LD11 required reading on Vietnam under communism.
- **Core argument:** Vietnam and China are both single-party regimes with rapid growth, but Vietnam's broader and more competitive elite institutions constrain leaders and force more redistributive public spending. This explains Vietnam's lower inequality better than economic, socio-cultural, or macro-regime-type explanations.
- **Named concepts/theories:** authoritarian institutional variation, winning coalition theory, elite accountability, Central Committee vs. Politburo, equalizing transfers, semicompetitive internal elections, redistributive public spending.
- **Cases/countries:** Vietnam and China.
- **Exam-useful vocabulary:** CCOM, Politburo, PBSC, General Secretary, troika, equalizing transfers, public expenditures, Gini coefficient, vertical checks, party congress, broad coalition.
- **Lecture tie:** LD11 follows the article closely: usual suspects predict Vietnam should be more unequal, so the answer is internal party hierarchy and coalition breadth.
