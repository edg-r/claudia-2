## Notes on the argument

The core puzzle is that Vietnam and China are both fast-growing single-party regimes, but China has seen much sharper inequality growth. Malesky, Abrami, and Zheng argue that the difference is not best explained by growth, culture, rural population, global integration, or communist ideology. The key difference is institutional: Vietnam's Central Committee is larger, more representative of provincial interests, and more electorally competitive inside the party than China's more concentrated Politburo-centered system.

That broader coalition changes policy incentives. Vietnamese leaders need to satisfy more regional and institutional actors, so the state spends more on equalizing transfers to poorer provinces. This helps explain why Vietnam has kept inequality more moderate even while growing quickly.

The question connects directly to the course's institutions thread. Ravanilla's broader frame is not just whether a regime is democratic or authoritarian, but how political institutions structure incentives. Vietnam complicates the easy story because some accountability and redistribution can emerge inside a single-party regime, even without open democratic competition.
