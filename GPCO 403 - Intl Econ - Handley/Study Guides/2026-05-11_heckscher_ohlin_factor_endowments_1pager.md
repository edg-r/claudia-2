## Core Idea

The Heckscher-Ohlin model explains trade by **differences in factor endowments**, not technology. Countries export the good that uses their **abundant factor** intensively and import the good that uses their **scarce factor** intensively (Feenstra & Taylor, 2021). If Canada is capital-abundant and India is labor-abundant, and robots are capital-intensive while rugs are labor-intensive, Canada should export robots and India should export rugs.

## Know These Terms Cold

- **Factor abundance:** A country has relatively more of a factor than another country, usually measured as capital per worker, land per worker, skilled labor share, etc.
- **Factor intensity:** A good uses one factor relatively more than another good. Robots are usually capital-intensive; handmade rugs are usually labor-intensive.
- **Autarky relative price:** The no-trade price ratio. The country with the lower autarky relative price of a good has comparative advantage in that good.
- **Trade triangle:** The gap between production and consumption under trade. One side is exports; the other is imports.
- **Leontief paradox:** The empirical puzzle that U.S. exports appeared less capital-intensive than U.S. imports, contrary to the simple HO prediction.
- **Factor-intensity reversal:** A good can be labor-intensive in one country and capital-intensive in another if production technology differs.

## The Model Logic

1. Identify which country is factor-abundant.
2. Identify which good is factor-intensive.
3. Match abundance to intensity: abundant factor + intensive good = export sector.
4. Use relative prices: trade raises the relative price of the export good and lowers the relative price of the import good.
5. Apply distributional effects: the factor used intensively in the rising-price sector wins; the other factor loses.

## Distribution: Stolper-Samuelson

The key political-economy punchline is that trade creates winners and losers inside the country. If the relative price of the capital-intensive good rises, the real return to capital rises and the real wage falls. If the relative price of the labor-intensive good rises, the real wage rises and the real return to capital falls. So trade conflict is often **factor conflict**, not just country-versus-country conflict.

## Exam-Style Moves

- If asked "who exports what," write: country abundance -> good intensity -> HO theorem -> export/import prediction.
- If asked about wages/rents, write: trade changes relative goods prices -> Stolper-Samuelson -> abundant factor gains, scarce factor loses.
- If asked to draw, put the capital-intensive good on one axis and show the capital-abundant country's PPF biased toward that good.
- If asked about China accumulating capital, show its PPF expanding more toward the capital-intensive good, lowering its no-trade relative price of that good and changing world import demand.
- If asked about Leontief, say the simple HO model can fail empirically because factor categories, technology, human capital, trade costs, and production methods are more complicated than the two-factor model.

## Quick Self-Test

1. Japan imports rice, and rice is labor-intensive. Is Japan labor-abundant or capital-abundant?
2. If the price of a capital-intensive export rises, what happens to real wages and real rental rates?
3. Why does a lower autarky relative price signal comparative advantage?
4. What would make a "labor-intensive" good become capital-intensive in another country?
