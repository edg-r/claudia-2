**Today's likely focus:** LD19, "How is the U.S.-China rivalry reshaping Southeast Asia?" The reading bundle shifts the course from domestic institutions and state capacity to regional order: South China Sea bargaining, ASEAN unity, influence competition, and U.S. economic credibility.

**BLUF:** Southeast Asian states are not simply choosing Washington or Beijing. They are hedging across issue areas while ASEAN's consensus rules and China's bilateral leverage make collective constraint difficult. The core tension is institutional: ASEAN can manage rivalry through process diplomacy, but weak consensus and unequal dependence make binding regional rules hard to enforce.

**Key Concepts**

- **Code of Conduct:** proposed South China Sea rules meant to manage or constrain behavior.
- **ASEAN consensus:** unity norm that preserves cohesion but lets one state block stronger action.
- **Bilateral leverage:** China's advantage when negotiating one-on-one with smaller states.
- **Relative influence:** RAND's idea that U.S. power matters in comparison with what China can offer or threaten.
- **Hedging:** Southeast Asian states keep options open, often mixing Chinese economic ties with U.S. security ties.
- **Economic statecraft:** Cutler/ASPI's point that trade rules, CPTPP/TPP, and market access are strategic tools, not just economic policy.

**Discussion-Ready Points**

2. Quang is more constructive: Vietnam's ASEAN chairmanship and an "ASEAN minus X" approach could help littoral states coordinate despite bloc-wide consensus problems.
3. RAND reframes influence as behavior-shaping, not vibes or prestige. The question is whether U.S. or Chinese tools actually change partner choices.
4. Cutler/ASPI adds the economic missing piece: U.S. security presence is weaker without credible trade engagement after TPP withdrawal.
5. Ravanilla's course lens still fits: institutions matter. Here, the key institutions are ASEAN rules, UNCLOS, trade architecture, and domestic credibility constraints in the U.S. and ASEAN states.

## GPCO 410 -- International Politics & Security

**Today's likely focus:** Week 10, public opinion and international security. The syllabus lists Rogowski, Fearon, and Herrmann/Tetlock/Visser. Treat this as the course capstone: strategic choice still matters, but leaders choose strategies through domestic audiences, public opinion, and institutional constraints.

**BLUF:** Today's big move is to connect domestic politics back to the strategic-choice framework. Fearon shows how public threats can create audience costs and make resolve credible. Herrmann/Tetlock/Visser show that public support for war is not automatic; it depends on how citizen dispositions interact with images of the case. Rogowski closes the loop by arguing that institutions aggregate preferences, create veto points, and constrain feasible foreign-policy strategies even when material pressures point elsewhere.

**Key Concepts**

- **Audience costs:** Domestic penalties for publicly committing and then backing down.
- **Tying hands:** A signal that is credible because retreat becomes politically costly.
- **Public opinion as structured judgment:** Citizens use dispositions, knowledge, and images of the adversary, not just raw cost-benefit calculation.
- **Institutional constraint:** Rules, veto points, and aggregation mechanisms that narrow what leaders can choose.
- **Institutional persistence:** Inefficient institutions may survive long enough to shape strategy.
- **Strategic choice grammar:** Actors, preferences, strategies, beliefs, payoffs, and why no bargain or deal worked.

**Discussion-Ready Points**

1. Fearon is not saying democracies are always peaceful. He is saying public commitments can help democracies reveal resolve faster because backing down is politically costly.
2. Herrmann/Tetlock/Visser complicate simple "public supports/opposes war" claims. The same conflict cue can move different publics differently depending on assertiveness, internationalism, knowledge, and adversary image.
3. Rogowski's useful middle ground: institutions are neither all-powerful nor irrelevant. Over the relevant decision horizon, sticky rules can constrain strategy even if they are endogenous in the long run.
4. For the final exam, use today's material to explain why leaders may not choose the materially obvious strategy: domestic audiences punish retreat, citizens interpret threats through case images, and institutions create veto points.

## GPPS 444 -- History of Warfare

**Today's likely focus:** Session 19, "Future of Warfare I." TCHW Epilogue: orbital warfare, counter-space operations, surveillance, precision warfare, and the limits of high-tech Western warfare.

**BLUF:** Parker's Epilogue argues that the Western way of war remains brutally effective in conventional combat because it combines technology, finance, discipline, adaptation, and global logistics. But the future battlefield exposes its weak points: satellites and networks can be attacked, precision systems depend on fragile supply chains, and asymmetric actors avoid the decisive battle the West is built to win. Thomas will likely want the bridge from "RMA dominance" to "political and logistical vulnerability."

**Key Concepts**

- **RMA / system of systems:** ISR, C4I, and precision-guided weapons produce lopsided conventional battlefield dominance.
- **Counter-space warfare:** Satellites are no longer background infrastructure; they are targetable military assets.
- **Surveillance as combat power:** Seeing, targeting, and networking forces can matter as much as platform lethality.
- **Asymmetric adaptation:** Weak actors bypass Western strengths through insurgency, terrorism, urban defense, cheap weapons, and political-will attacks.
- **Sinews of war:** Military power depends on money, supply chains, public tolerance for casualties, and political will.
- **Unequal dialogue:** Civilian leaders must keep military operations tied to political objectives.

**Discussion-Ready Points**

1. The next offense-defense balance may turn on visibility. Precision favors the side that can see and target first, but counter-space, cyber, camouflage, deception, and urban terrain can restore defensive advantage.
2. The RMA is not just weapons; it is an ecosystem. Satellites, chips, software, allied suppliers, command networks, and trained operators are all part of the weapon.
3. Mazar-i-Sharif is useful for class today because it shows future-war themes already visible in 2001: small teams, local allies, airpower, intelligence, and precision strikes, but also the problem of translating tactical success into stable politics.
4. Operation Paraquet and Lang Son raise the older lesson in modern dress: expeditionary operations still depend on logistics, terrain, leadership, and political purpose. Technology does not erase geography.
5. The course arc comes full circle: from hoplite discipline and Roman organization to satellites and AI, Thomas's core questions stay the same: who can mass effects, move, adapt, supply, and lead under pressure?

## GPCO 403 -- International Economics

**Today's likely focus:** Trade Policy II and synthesis across trade models and policy.

**BLUF:** Today is probably a synthesis class: move from "what tariffs do in a diagram" to "how trade policy works in institutions and evidence." Be ready to connect welfare areas from Ch. 8 to WTO rules, PTAs, trade creation/diversion, and the empirical lesson from the 2018 tariffs: the U.S. did not meaningfully shift tariff costs onto foreign exporters in the short run.

**Key Concepts**

- **Small-country tariff:** Domestic price rises by the full tariff; consumer surplus falls; producer surplus and government revenue rise; national welfare falls by deadweight loss.
- **Large-country tariff:** Possible terms-of-trade gain if foreign exporters lower pre-tariff prices, but retaliation can erase gains.
- **Quotas / VERs:** Same price/quantity effects as equivalent tariffs, but quota rents depend on who gets licenses; VERs can be worse if rents go abroad.
- **WTO/GATT logic:** MFN prevents discrimination across trading partners; national treatment prevents discrimination against imports after entry.
- **PTAs:** Trade creation improves efficiency; trade diversion can reduce welfare by shifting imports from a lower-cost nonmember to a higher-cost partner.
- **Amiti, Redding, Weinstein:** 2018 tariffs had near-complete pass-through to U.S. buyers, about $3 billion per month in added tax costs, $1.4 billion per month in deadweight loss, and major trade redirection.

**Discussion-Ready Points**

1. A tariff is not just "helping domestic producers"; it redistributes from consumers and may destroy surplus. The key question is whether any terms-of-trade gain exists and whether it survives retaliation.
2. The 2018 tariff evidence weakens the simple large-country optimism: even a large importer may face sticky supply chains and differentiated products that make pass-through land on domestic firms/consumers.
3. Quotas are politically attractive because the cost is less visible, but the welfare outcome can be worse than a tariff if rents are captured through lobbying or by foreign exporters.
4. WTO rules are best understood as a commitment device against tariff-war incentives, not as abstract legalism. MFN and national treatment operationalize non-discrimination.
5. PTAs are not automatically "free trade." They liberalize inside the bloc while discriminating against outsiders, so welfare depends on trade creation versus diversion.
