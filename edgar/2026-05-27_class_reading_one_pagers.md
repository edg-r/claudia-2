# International Politics & Security

## Lake, "Building Legitimate States"

**Core question:** Why do some statebuilding projects fail even when outside actors can provide force, money, and institutions?

**Main argument:** Lake argues that statebuilding begins with legitimacy. A state is not just an organization with coercive capacity; it is an organization whose right to rule is collectively accepted by the relevant community. State failure occurs when that legitimacy breaks down, usually through deep factionalization. The task for statebuilders is therefore not simply to impose order, write a constitution, or train security forces. It is to help create a political order that local groups find self-enforcing enough to support over time.

**Key concepts:**

- **Legitimacy:** Collective acceptance of a ruler's right to rule.
- **Quasi-voluntary compliance:** People comply partly by choice and partly because noncompliance can be sanctioned.
- **Social order:** Protection of persons, property, and promises.
- **Self-enforcing institutions:** Rules that major actors continue to support because doing so serves their interests.

**Mechanism:** Coercion alone is too expensive and unstable. A ruler cannot keep force everywhere all at once. Legitimacy bridges coercion and consent by making people accept that commands are binding even when they dislike specific policies. If enough people accept the state's authority, the state can punish free riders and dissenters without relying on constant direct force.

**Statebuilder's dilemma:** External actors may be able to catalyze a new order, but legitimacy cannot simply be imported. Foreign statebuilders must get social factions invested in the new arrangement, then credibly guarantee it long enough for those factions to stop hedging, fighting, or defecting. The harder the factional split, the harder it is to make a new order self-enforcing.

**Class hook:** If legitimacy rests on collective acceptance, can foreign intervention ever build a legitimate state, or can it only create a temporary security umbrella while local actors decide whether to buy into the new order?

# International Economics

## Feenstra & Taylor Ch. 8, 11.1-11.2; Amiti, Redding & Weinstein (2019)

**Core question:** Who gains and who loses from trade policy, and why do tariffs often impose costs beyond the protected industry?

**Main argument:** These readings shift from the gains from trade to the politics and welfare effects of restricting trade. Tariffs can protect selected domestic producers, but they raise prices for consumers and for firms that use imported inputs. The central economic point is incidence: the legal target of a tariff is not necessarily the party that bears its full burden. The Amiti, Redding, and Weinstein study of the 2018 U.S. tariffs shows that much of the tariff cost was passed through into U.S. prices, meaning domestic consumers and firms absorbed a substantial share.

**Key ideas:**

- **Tariff:** A tax on imports that raises the domestic price of the imported good.
- **Consumer loss:** Consumers pay higher prices and buy less.
- **Producer gain:** Protected domestic firms may sell more or receive higher prices.
- **Government revenue:** Tariffs can raise revenue, but this does not automatically offset welfare losses.
- **Deadweight loss:** Efficiency loss from distorted consumption and production decisions.
- **Input-output linkages:** Tariffs on intermediate goods can raise costs for domestic producers downstream.

**Distributional politics:** Trade policy creates concentrated benefits and diffuse costs. A protected industry may have strong incentives to lobby, while consumers each lose a smaller amount and are harder to organize. This helps explain why inefficient protection can persist politically.

**2018 tariff evidence:** Amiti, Redding, and Weinstein find that tariffs increased prices and reduced welfare. The costs did not stay neatly with foreign exporters. Instead, U.S. consumers and importing firms faced higher prices, while retaliatory tariffs hurt U.S. exporters. The result is a good empirical example of why trade wars can spread costs through supply chains.

**Class hook:** If tariffs are economically costly but politically attractive, should we think of them mainly as bad economics, or as a predictable response to concentrated political pressure and distributional conflict?

# Quantitative Methods 3

## Gerber & Green, Field Experiments Ch. 4

**Core question:** What can go wrong after random assignment, and how should researchers estimate causal effects when subjects do not comply with treatment?

**Main argument:** Field experiments are powerful because random assignment creates comparable treatment and control groups. But real subjects often do not behave exactly as assigned. Some assigned to treatment do not receive it, some assigned to control may get exposed anyway, and treatments can interact with other features of the environment. Chapter 4 focuses on this practical problem: causal inference must distinguish the effect of being **assigned** treatment from the effect of actually **receiving** treatment.

**Key concepts:**

- **Intention-to-treat (ITT):** Effect of assignment to treatment, regardless of whether treatment was actually received.
- **Treatment-on-the-treated (TOT):** Effect among those who actually receive the treatment because of assignment.
- **Noncompliance:** When assigned treatment status and actual treatment receipt differ.
- **Encouragement design:** Assignment changes the probability of treatment uptake rather than forcing treatment directly.
- **Factorial design:** Randomly vary more than one treatment dimension to estimate separate and interactive effects.

**Why ITT matters:** ITT preserves the original randomization. Even if some people do not comply, assignment was random, so comparing assigned treatment and assigned control groups still gives a clean causal estimate of the offer, encouragement, or program rollout.

**Why TOT is harder:** Actual treatment receipt is usually not random. People who comply may differ systematically from people who do not. To estimate TOT, researchers often use assignment as an instrument for treatment receipt. This requires strong assumptions, especially that assignment affects outcomes only through treatment uptake.

**Design lesson:** Good field experiments anticipate implementation problems. Researchers should measure take-up, contamination, attrition, and spillovers; pre-specify estimands; and decide whether the policy-relevant effect is the effect of offering a program or the effect of actual participation.

**Class hook:** In policy terms, is ITT sometimes more useful than TOT because governments can usually control who is offered a program, but not who actually takes it up?

# History of Warfare

## Parker, The Cambridge History of Warfare, Epilogue

**Core question:** How did nuclear weapons, precision strike, and information technology transform the Western way of war after 1945?

**Main argument:** The epilogue extends the course's long arc of Western warfare into the nuclear and post-Cold War era. World War II ended with the atomic bomb, making the destructive potential of modern science impossible to ignore. During the Cold War, nuclear deterrence prevented direct U.S.-Soviet war, but it also redirected military planning toward escalation management, delivery systems, and technological superiority. By the Gulf War, the U.S. demonstrated a new form of conventional dominance built around stealth, cruise missiles, electronic warfare, precision-guided munitions, and joint operations.

**Key developments:**

- **Nuclear deterrence:** Great-power war became far more dangerous because escalation could become catastrophic.
- **Science and military power:** Research, engineering, and systems integration became central to military advantage.
- **Cold War rearmament:** Korea and the Soviet threat drove sustained U.S. investment in conventional and nuclear capability.
- **Vietnam lesson:** Technological superiority did not guarantee political success in a guerrilla war.
- **Precision warfare:** By 1991, the U.S. could attack command, infrastructure, and battlefield targets with unprecedented accuracy.

**Continuity with the course:** The Western way of war still rests on technology, discipline, finance, and institutional learning. But the epilogue shows a shift from mass industrial warfare toward high-tech systems that combine sensors, command networks, airpower, and precision strike.

**Limits:** Precision does not solve every military problem. It can destroy targets efficiently, but it may not produce political settlement, legitimacy, occupation capacity, or long-term stability. The Gulf War's battlefield success left Saddam Hussein in power, showing the difference between operational victory and strategic resolution.

**Class hook:** Did precision warfare represent a true revolution in war, or mainly a new version of the older Western pattern of turning wealth, organization, and technology into battlefield advantage?

# Politics of Southeast Asia

## Tajima, Samphantharak & Ostwald (2018), "Ethnic Segregation and Public Goods"

**Core question:** Is ethnic and racial diversity itself bad for Southeast Asia, or does the effect depend on how groups are spatially organized and how local governments allocate resources?

**Main argument:** The article complicates the common "diversity penalty" argument. Prior political economy work often finds that ethnically diverse areas provide fewer public goods because diversity makes collective action harder. Tajima, Samphantharak, and Ostwald agree that ethnic fractionalization can hurt public goods provision, but they argue that **spatial segregation** changes the story. When ethnic groups are distributed into relatively homogeneous villages within a diverse district, those villages may organize more effectively and lobby district officials for resources.

**Key mechanism:** The authors distinguish between the **decision level** and the **user level**. District officials often decide where public goods go, but villages are where people actually use schools, clinics, roads, and other goods. A district can be diverse overall while its villages are either integrated or segregated. Homogeneous villages may solve local coordination problems more easily and then use public goods in nearby villages as leverage to demand matching investments. The authors call this **spatial interdependence**.

**Evidence:** Using Indonesian census and village data from around 2000, the authors examine more than 50,000 villages. They find that segregation is positively associated with public goods where district officials had discretion over placement: middle schools, high schools, health centers, and asphalt roads. For goods shaped more by national formula-based programs, such as primary schools and health subcenters, segregation does not have the same association.

**Why it matters:** The finding does not mean segregation is good. Segregation may help local advocacy, but it can also reduce interethnic contact, reinforce group boundaries, and weaken national identity. The policy problem is that decentralization may unintentionally reward segregated communities and penalize integrated ones unless higher levels of government compensate.

**Class hook:** If integration is normatively attractive but segregation can make local advocacy more effective, how should Southeast Asian states design decentralization and redistribution policy?
