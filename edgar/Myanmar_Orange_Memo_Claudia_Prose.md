# The Transition Trap: Democratization and Governmental Conflict in Myanmar

## Introduction
The conventional wisdom of international relations has long championed democratization as a pacifying force, arguing that democratic institutions channel societal grievances into peaceful electoral competition. However, this optimistic view ignores the volatile dynamics of the transition itself. Regime change is rarely a linear, harmonious progression; rather, it is a highly contested reordering of power that often destabilizes the state. In their seminal work, Lars-Erik Cederman, Simon Hug, and Andreas Krebs (2010) demonstrate that the process of democratization—rather than the end state of stable democracy—significantly heightens the risk of civil war. When applied to the tragic trajectory of Myanmar from 2010 to 2021, this theoretical framework reveals that the country’s managed transition was structurally predisposed to violence. This essay argues that Myanmar’s democratic transition dramatically heightened the risk of internal conflict, culminating in the 2021 military coup and subsequent civil war, because the rapid mobilization of the civilian population under the National League for Democracy (NLD) outpaced the capacity of hybrid constitutional structures to manage the contest over national authority—a classic manifestation of Cederman, Hug, and Krebs’s model of transition-induced governmental conflict.

## Context
For over half a century following its independence, Myanmar (formerly Burma) was ruled by a highly repressive military junta, the Tatmadaw, which consolidated power through physical coercion and the systematic exclusion of civilian voices. In 2008, seeking to break its international isolation and formalize its political dominance under a veneer of legitimacy, the military drafted a new constitution that established a system of "disciplined, flourishing democracy." This hybrid framework, which initiated a transition in 2010, was deliberately designed to protect the military's core interests. Under the 2008 Constitution, the military reserved crucial institutional safeguards: it held a permanent 25% legislative block of unelected seats in both houses of parliament, retained direct control over key security ministries (Defense, Border Affairs, and Home Affairs), and maintained a constitutional veto over amendments, which required more than 75% of parliamentary support (Article 436). 

The political landscape shifted dramatically during the 2015 general election, when the National League for Democracy (NLD), led by Nobel laureate Aung San Suu Kyi, won a historic landslide victory. The NLD entered a uneasy co-governance arrangement with the Tatmadaw, with Aung San Suu Kyi serving as State Counsellor. While this arrangement maintained a fragile peace for five years, the fundamental structural tensions remained unresolved. The civilian government sought to gradually reform the constitution to establish civilian supremacy, while the military, led by Senior General Min Aung Hlaing, viewed any erosion of its constitutional veto as an existential threat. The polarization reached a breaking point following the November 2020 elections, where the NLD won an even larger landslide victory, decimating the military’s proxy political party, the Union Solidarity and Development Party (USDP). Fearing that its managed democracy would no longer secure its veto, the Tatmadaw launched a coup on February 1, 2021, arresting civilian leaders and sparking a nationwide armed resistance.

## Theory
The violent collapse of Myanmar's hybrid regime directly validates the strategic and empirical logic articulated by Cederman, Hug, and Krebs (2010). The authors present a robust quantitative critique of the democratic peace theory, demonstrating that states undergoing democratization experience a civil war risk **2.77 times higher** than stable regimes. Crucially, Cederman et al. draw a vital distinction regarding the nature of this conflict: transition-induced instability is overwhelmingly concentrated in **governmental conflicts** (contests over who controls the central state apparatus) rather than **territorial conflicts** (regional secession or autonomy struggles). 

The primary mechanism driving this risk is the *mobilization-institutionalization lag*, an concept rooted in Huntingtonian political theory. During democratization, the opening of political space mobilizes societal groups and generates demands for political participation at a pace that far exceeds the capacity of weak or transitioning state institutions to absorb and arbitrate. In Myanmar, the opening of the political system after 2010 mobilized the civilian population on an unprecedented scale. Aung San Suu Kyi and the NLD successfully transformed this popular mobilization into overwhelming electoral legitimacy. 

Applying the strategic choice framework, this electoral mobilization functioned as a dynamic, power-shifting asset. Under the 2008 Constitution, the rules of the game were highly distributive; the consensus rule (Article 436) protected the Tatmadaw's permanent veto over the state. However, the NLD's consecutive landslide victories in 2015 and 2020 signaled an inevitable and rapid shift in future political power. As the NLD's domestic and international legitimacy grew, the military realized that its constitutional veto was no longer secure. 

This dynamic created an acute commitment problem under complete information. Aung San Suu Kyi could not credibly commit to preserving the military's permanent institutional autonomy and economic privileges in the future, as her civilian constituency demanded full constitutional reform and civilian supremacy. Conversely, the Tatmadaw could not credibly commit to respecting the civilian government's authority as long as the military maintained a monopoly on physical force and reserved security portfolios. Because the NLD's electoral legitimacy was a dynamic asset that threatened to permanently alter the future distribution of power, the military chose to preempt this anticipated power shift. The resulting 2021 coup was not a dispute over regional borders or ethnic secession (territorial conflict), but a direct, zero-sum contest over control of the national government (governmental conflict), perfectly illustrating the Cederman et al. transition model.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, Myanmar’s descent into systemic civil war post-2021 demonstrates the profound dangers inherent in regime transitions where societal mobilization outpaces institutional consolidation. By applying the theoretical insights of Cederman, Hug, and Krebs (2010), we can see that the conflict was not an accidental miscalculation, but a structurally driven breakdown of a hybrid co-governance agreement under the pressure of shifting power. While the Cederman et al. framework offers powerful predictive capabilities, it does suffer from certain limitations. Its reliance on broad, country-level datasets like Polity IV can conflate transition indicators with actual conflict precursors, and it struggles to fully capture the complex, pre-existing ethnic-regional dynamics (territorial conflicts) that in Myanmar's case became deeply entangled with the primary governmental struggle. Nevertheless, the Myanmar case remains a stark warning for international statebuilders: the road to democracy is paved with structural landmines, proving that in the delicate theater of political transition, opening the gates of participation before reinforcing the pillars of institutional compromise is a surefire recipe for state collapse.
