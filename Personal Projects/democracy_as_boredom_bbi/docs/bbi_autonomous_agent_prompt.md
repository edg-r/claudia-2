## 2. Core Research Question

Answer the following research question:

**Can presidential speeches be scored for “bureaucratic boredom” as a proxy for the rhetorical presence of institutional constraint?**

The project should develop and test a text-based measure called the:

**Bureaucratic Boredom Index (BBI)**

The index should capture the degree to which presidential speech relies on procedural, institutional, administrative, legalistic language rather than charismatic, personalistic, existential, or sovereignty-centered language.

The initial formula should be:

**BBI = standardized procedural constraint language score minus standardized charismatic sovereignty language score**

The goal is not to prove that the BBI directly measures democracy by itself. The goal is to test whether the BBI captures a **rhetorical trace of institutional constraint**.

### 4.1 Rhetorical Presidency and Long-Run Presidential Speech Change

Use the rhetorical presidency literature to show that presidential speech has already been analyzed as a measurable historical corpus.

Key sources:

- Lim (2002) uses computer-assisted content analysis on U.S. inaugural addresses and annual messages from 1789 to 2000. He argues that presidential rhetoric became more anti-intellectual, abstract, assertive, democratic, and conversational. This source is important because it shows that presidential rhetoric changes systematically over time.
- Savoy (2017) analyzes U.S. presidential style and rhetoric over two centuries using stylometric and computational techniques, including work on State of the Union addresses and inaugural addresses. This source is useful because it supports treating presidential speech as a long-run measurable corpus.

Connection to this project:

Unlike these works, this project does not measure presidential style for its own sake. It measures whether speech contains the procedural language expected when executives are rhetorically constrained by democratic institutions.

### 4.2 Populist Discourse and People-versus-Elite Rhetoric

Use the populism literature to show that political speech can be coded for claims about the people, elites, corruption, crisis, and legitimacy.

Key sources:

- Bonikowski and Gidron (2016) measure populist style in U.S. presidential campaign rhetoric from 1952 to 1996. Their key methodological move is to treat populism as a measurable attribute of speech rather than a fixed identity of a leader.
- Bonikowski, Luo, and Stuhler (2022) extend the measurement of populism, nationalism, and authoritarianism in U.S. presidential campaigns from 1952 to 2020 using neural language models.
- The Global Populism Database measures populist discourse in speeches by presidents and prime ministers across countries. It is a precedent for cross-national coding of leader speech.

Connection to this project:

The BBI should follow the same general logic: treat democratic constraint not as a fixed label attached to a leader, but as a measurable rhetorical attribute of specific speeches.

### 4.3 Authoritarian, Illiberal, and Personalist Rhetoric

Use the authoritarian discourse and personalism literature to show that leader speech can indicate democratic erosion or weakening executive constraints.

Key sources:

- Mochtak (2025) develops a deep-learning approach to detecting authoritarian discourse in political speeches, using United Nations General Assembly speech data and V-Dem polyarchy labels.
- Brunkert and von Soest (2023) study personalist legitimation strategies and find that personalist legitimacy claims can warn of later deterioration in executive constraints, especially weaker judicial and legislative oversight.
- Recent work on an “Illiberal Speech Index” suggests that leader language may provide early warning signs of democratic decline across many countries and administrations.

Connection to this project:

Most of this literature looks for dangerous or anti-democratic language. The BBI flips the perspective. It measures whether leaders acknowledge institutional constraint through procedural, administrative, and legalistic language.

### 4.4 Text-as-Data Methods and Cross-National Speech Corpora

Key sources:
