## Introduction

NATO's accession of Finland and Sweden is best explained as a regime adjustment after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The invasion changed the security payoff of staying outside NATO, especially for Finland and Sweden. But expansion was not automatic. NATO's consensus rule meant every member had a veto, and Turkey used that rule to bargain over its own security concerns. Arthur Stein's theory helps explain both parts: states move from independent decision-making to joint decision-making when acting alone becomes too risky or inefficient.[^1]

## Context

Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022. Finland and Sweden applied to join NATO in May 2022. This was a major shift because both states had long traditions of staying outside the alliance. Their decision was not just symbolic. Joining NATO meant accepting Article 5, integrated defense planning, and future obligations to other members.

Turkey then objected to their accession. Its main concerns involved Kurdish groups, extradition, security cooperation, and arms restrictions. Turkey, Finland, and Sweden signed a trilateral memorandum in Madrid on June 28, 2022, and NATO allies signed the accession protocols on July 5, 2022.[^2] Finland joined NATO on April 4, 2023, and Sweden joined on March 7, 2024.[^3]

## Theory

Stein argues that regimes are not just international organizations. A regime exists when patterned state behavior comes from joint decision-making rather than fully independent national choices.[^4] This fits NATO because members keep sovereignty, but they also accept rules and expectations that shape how they respond to threats.

Stein separates two problems. In dilemmas of common aversions, states may not want the same perfect outcome, but they all want to avoid a bad one. For NATO, the bad outcome was alliance division and more Russian leverage in Europe. In dilemmas of common interests, states want cooperation, but still have incentives to shift costs or bargain for better terms. NATO burden-sharing and Article 5 credibility fit this second problem. Members want deterrence, but no state wants unlimited obligations without influence over the rules.

## Application

For Finland and Sweden, Russia's invasion made nonalignment less attractive. Before 2022, staying outside NATO preserved flexibility. After Ukraine, that flexibility looked more dangerous. Finland's long border with Russia made this especially clear. The choice was no longer between neutrality and unnecessary entanglement. It was between independent defense under greater threat and joint defense under NATO.

Sweden faced a similar calculation. Its nonalignment had value when the regional environment was more stable. Once Russia showed a willingness to use large-scale force, Sweden had more reason to lock in allied support. In Stein's terms, both states accepted a regime that constrained future choices in exchange for more predictable security commitments.

Turkey is the harder and more interesting part of the case. Its objection was not simply anti-NATO behavior. It was bargaining inside NATO's regime. Because NATO enlargement requires consensus, Turkey had leverage before Finland and Sweden could enter. It used that leverage to press for policy concessions and recognition of its own security priorities.

This shows why regimes do not erase national preferences. They channel them. Turkey shared the general aversion to alliance weakness and Russian coercion, but it did not share the same ranking of every issue. For Turkey, Kurdish groups and arms restrictions mattered enough to delay accession. The Madrid memorandum helped align expectations enough for the process to continue.

Gourevitch adds a useful caveat here. If institutions matter, then actors will fight over institutional rules because rules shape outcomes.[^5] Turkey's veto power shows that point. NATO's consensus rule helped preserve unity by requiring every member to agree, but it also gave one member a chance to slow the process and extract concessions.

## Conclusion

NATO expansion was therefore both a success and a warning. Stein explains why Finland and Sweden moved toward joint security decision-making after Russia changed the payoff structure. Turkey's objections show that this cooperation still had politics inside it. The expansion strengthened NATO if the deterrent value of Finland and Sweden outweighed the bargaining costs of unanimity. It was not automatic solidarity. It was cooperation through rules, pressure, and negotiated consent.
