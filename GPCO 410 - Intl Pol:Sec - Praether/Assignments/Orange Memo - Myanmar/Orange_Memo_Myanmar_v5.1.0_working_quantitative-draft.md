## Introduction

Myanmar was at greater risk of political violence and civil war after the 2010 opening because democratization changed the struggle over governmental power without taking coercive power away from the military. The reforms created elections, party competition, and public mobilization, but they did not create credible civilian control over the Tatmadaw. Cederman, Hug, and Krebs's quantitative finding helps explain why this mattered. Their argument is not that stable democracy causes civil war. It is that periods of democratization raise civil-war risk because political competition expands before institutions can settle who controls the state. Myanmar fits that logic. The 2021 coup was not a separate event from the 2010 opening. It was the moment when the military rejected a transition that increasingly threatened its preferred position.

## Context

The key actors were Aung San Suu Kyi and the National League for Democracy on one side, and Senior General Min Aung Hlaing and the Tatmadaw on the other. The NLD preferred to convert electoral support into real governing authority. It wanted civilian legitimacy to matter not just at the ballot box, but inside the state. The Tatmadaw preferred institutional autonomy, protection from civilian oversight, and a reserved role in politics. It accepted limited liberalization only while the transition preserved military veto power.

This is why the 2010 reforms were dangerous rather than simply stabilizing. President Thein Sein's government released Aung San Suu Kyi and opened space for political participation, but the transition stayed military-managed. The 2008 constitutional order left the Tatmadaw with reserved seats, control over major security ministries, and enough leverage to block reforms that threatened military autonomy. The 2015 NLD landslide changed who had democratic legitimacy. It did not change who controlled force. The 2020 NLD landslide made the same problem sharper. Open elections showed that the military-backed order could not reliably win popular consent, while the military still had the coercive capacity to reject the result.

## Theory

Cederman, Hug, and Krebs separate regime type from regime change. This is the most important theoretical point for Myanmar. Their empirical evidence shows that democratization has a strong and robust association with civil-war onset. In their logit models, the democratization coefficient is positive and statistically significant, with a coefficient of about 1.018 in Table I. They also estimate that democratization raises the probability of civil-war onset by about 0.038, holding other factors constant. The size of the estimate matters less than the direction and robustness of the finding: transition periods are risky in their own right.

Their lagged results matter too. In Table II, democratization remains significant when measured over the current year and the previous three years. That makes the theory useful for Myanmar because the violence did not explode immediately in 2010. The opening created a slower political process. The NLD gained legitimacy, citizens became more mobilized around elections, and the military's expected future control declined. The danger was cumulative. Democratization made the contest over the state more public and more threatening over time.

Cederman, Hug, and Krebs also distinguish governmental conflict from territorial conflict. Territorial conflict is fought over regional autonomy or secession. Governmental conflict is fought over who controls the national government. Myanmar already had long-running territorial conflicts involving ethnic armed organizations. The democratic opening added a more direct governmental conflict between elected civilian authority and military rule. That distinction keeps the theory anchored. The strongest Cederman point is not that all violence in Myanmar came from democratization. It is that democratization increased the risk of conflict over government.

## Application

The transition failed because the actors' preferences could not both be satisfied. Aung San Suu Kyi and the NLD wanted electoral victory to become civilian authority. Min Aung Hlaing and the Tatmadaw wanted elections to remain subordinate to military veto power. As long as the NLD's gains were limited, the military could tolerate partial democratization. But repeated landslides changed the expected distribution of future power. Continued democratization made the Tatmadaw look less like the guardian of the state and more like an institution losing public legitimacy.

That is why the 2021 coup should be read as part of the transition, not only as an autocratic shock after it. Fraud claims gave the military a public excuse, but the deeper conflict was over control of the government. The Tatmadaw still controlled coercion, so it could block the political outcome it disliked. The NLD had mass legitimacy, so the coup did not simply restore the old order. It produced resistance from a society that had already spent a decade learning to organize around elections, civilian rule, and democratic legitimacy.

The post-coup war shows the same pattern. Ethnic armed organizations still often fight territorial conflicts over autonomy and local control, but the National Unity Government and People's Defence Forces fight a governmental conflict over who has the right to rule Myanmar. Cederman, Hug, and Krebs's theory explains that center of gravity better than a simple democracy-versus-dictatorship story. Democratization raised political expectations and mobilized citizens, but the state never built institutions strong enough to make the military accept civilian supremacy.

## Conclusion

Myanmar was at greater risk of political violence and civil war after 2010 because the transition opened competition without resolving the coercive power problem. The country looked more stable for part of the reform decade, but that stability depended on the Tatmadaw's veto. Once NLD victories made civilian authority more plausible, the military chose autocratic reversal. Once the coup closed the electoral path, the opposition chose resistance. Myanmar supports Cederman, Hug, and Krebs because the danger came from democratization as a transition period, especially a transition that intensified conflict over government.
