## BLUF

The useful through-line is **strategic action under uncertainty**. Kydd and Walter show terrorism as costly signaling by weak actors. Stein shows why states sometimes replace independent self-help with regimes of joint decision-making. Gourevitch adds the warning: once institutions matter, actors fight over the rules themselves. For class, keep asking one question: **whose beliefs or expectations is the actor trying to change?**

## Reading Anchors

**Kydd & Walter:** terrorism is violence against civilians by nonstate actors to achieve political goals, but the core contribution is strategy, not definition. They identify five strategic logics of costly signaling: **attrition**, **intimidation**, **provocation**, **spoiling**, and **outbidding** (Kydd & Walter, 2006). The local PDF explicitly frames these as the five principal strategic logics and says understanding the distinctions is necessary for effective counterterrorism.

**Stein:** regimes exist when behavior is shaped by **joint rather than independent decision-making** (Stein, 1982). His key split is between **dilemmas of common interests**, which require collaboration and monitoring because actors may cheat, and **dilemmas of common aversions**, which require coordination because actors need convergent expectations to avoid a mutually bad outcome.

**Gourevitch:** institutions shape outcomes, so they become objects of struggle (Gourevitch, 1999). This is the **governance problem**: politics over contested institutions. Strong institutions can resolve disputes through rules; weak institutions leave actors to settle governance disputes through bargaining power, material resources, or violence.

## Kydd & Walter: Fast Strategy Diagnostic

| Strategy | Belief the terrorist group wants to change | Fast cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Attrition** | Target government believes continuing the policy is too costly. | Exhaust the enemy. |
| **Intimidation** | Civilians believe the group can punish disobedience and the state cannot protect them. | Control the population. |
| **Provocation** | Constituency believes the state is brutal or illegitimate after overreaction. | Trigger indiscriminate retaliation. |
| **Spoiling** | Target believes moderates cannot control extremists or enforce peace. | Wreck a settlement. |
| **Outbidding** | Supporters believe this group is more resolved than rival factions. | Beat rival groups for legitimacy. |

## Stein + Gourevitch: Collective Security Lens

- **Coordination problem:** actors need shared expectations. Example logic: "If we all know what counts as aggression and how we will respond, we avoid drift, panic, or accidental defection."
- **Collaboration problem:** actors need assurance against cheating or free riding. Example logic: "I will commit resources only if I believe others will also carry costs and cannot quietly defect."
- **Governance problem:** once rules distribute power and benefits, actors fight over the rules. Example logic: "The institution helps, but now the real fight is who controls its procedures, vetoes, thresholds, and enforcement."

## Class-Ready Synthesis

Terrorism and collective security look like opposite topics, but the mechanism is similar: actors try to manipulate expectations when direct control is impossible. Terrorist groups use violence to make an audience infer resolve, weakness, brutality, unreliability, or factional superiority. States use regimes to stabilize expectations and manage fear under anarchy. The danger is that institutions do not end strategic conflict. They change its arena. A counterterrorism policy can fail by answering the wrong terrorist strategy; a collective-security regime can fail by treating a governance dispute as if it were only a technical coordination problem.

## Good Discussion Questions

1. Which terrorism strategy is most likely to succeed against democracies: attrition, provocation, or outbidding?
2. When does a strong state response defeat terrorism, and when does it become part of the terrorist group's strategy?
3. Is collective security mostly a coordination problem, a collaboration problem, or a governance problem?
4. If institutions are contested because they matter, what would count as a "strong" institution in Stein and Gourevitch's terms?
