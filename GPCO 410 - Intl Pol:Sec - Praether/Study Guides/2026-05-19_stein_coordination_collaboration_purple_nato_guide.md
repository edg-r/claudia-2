## Core Claim

Stein's core move is definitional. A regime exists when patterned state behavior comes from **joint decision-making**, not merely parallel self-interest or membership in an organization. Regimes arise in anarchy when self-interested states face either **dilemmas of common interests** or **dilemmas of common aversions**. NATO is useful for the PURPLE memo because expansion after Russia's 2022 invasion shows both logics at once: Finland and Sweden wanted Article 5 protection and predictable allied coordination, while existing members had to decide whether adding them improved collective security enough to justify new obligations (NATO, 2024; Stein, 1982).

## Key Concepts

| Stein concept | Plain meaning | NATO memo use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Regime** | Joint decision-making that constrains independent state behavior. | NATO membership changes future choices: members cannot treat attacks on allies as someone else's problem. |
| **Common interests / collaboration** | Actors prefer mutual cooperation but have incentives to cheat or free ride. | Burden-sharing, defense spending, intelligence sharing, and Article 5 credibility all require members to keep commitments. |

| **Institution vs. regime** | An organization is not automatically a regime. | Do not say "NATO exists, therefore cooperation is solved." Show how rules and expectations actually shape choices. |

## Applying It: NATO Expansion and Turkey

Finland and Sweden's interest in joining is the easy part: Russia's invasion changed their preference ordering. Neutrality/nonalignment looked less safe; Article 5 and integrated defense planning looked more valuable. In Stein's terms, the war changed the **constellation of interests** enough to make joint decision-making preferable to independent security policy.

Existing members' commitments are the collaboration problem. Article 5 is valuable only if members believe others will treat an attack on one as a collective security issue. That means NATO expansion increases both security benefits and obligations. Members are not just admitting Finland and Sweden; they are expanding the set of states whose defense may require their resources, credibility, and risk acceptance.

Turkey's initial opposition is the memo's best analytical hinge. Do not frame it as Turkey being "anti-NATO." Frame it as a member using a consensus regime to bargain over a distributional and security dispute. Turkey shared the common aversion to alliance weakness and Russian leverage, but it had different preferences over Kurdish groups, extradition/security assurances, arms restrictions, and its own status inside the alliance. Stein helps explain why that did not destroy NATO: coordination regimes often involve public threats to defect as bargaining moves, not hidden cheating. Turkey's objection was visible, costly, and aimed at changing the equilibrium before accession was finalized.

## Usable Memo Moves

1. **Open with a Stein sentence:** "NATO expansion is best understood as a regime adjustment under anarchy: Finland and Sweden chose joint security decision-making after Russia made independent security policy less attractive."

2. **Separate motives from mechanism:** Finland and Sweden joined because threat perceptions changed; NATO could absorb them because the alliance already had a regime structure for coordinating expectations and enforcing commitments.

3. **Make Turkey the theory test:** Turkey's opposition shows that regimes do not erase national preferences. They channel conflict through rules, especially consensus, so members bargain inside the institution instead of abandoning it.

4. **Use the coordination/collaboration split:** Article 5 credibility and burden-sharing are collaboration problems; accession timing, diplomatic sequencing, and avoiding alliance fracture are coordination problems.

5. **End with a restrained claim:** The expansion strengthened NATO if it increased credible joint defense more than it increased bargaining frictions. Stein's framework lets Edgar evaluate that tradeoff without treating NATO as either automatic solidarity or empty symbolism.
