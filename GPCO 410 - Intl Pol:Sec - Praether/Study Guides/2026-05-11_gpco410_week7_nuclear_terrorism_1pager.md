## Core Question

How do actors make threats credible when the threatened action is either extremely costly, morally constrained, or politically risky? This week links nuclear coercion to suicide terrorism through the same strategic-choice problem: violence is not just force, it is a signal meant to change an opponent's beliefs about resolve, costs, and future concessions.

## Reading Map

**Carpenter & Bandow:** North Korea uses nuclear brinkmanship as leverage. The key idea is not simply that Pyongyang wants weapons for defense, but that nuclear ambiguity and escalation risk help it extract concessions while making U.S. military options costly. Their Korea case raises the credibility problem from the other side: if North Korea threatens escalation, does the United States believe it, and if the United States threatens punishment, does Pyongyang believe Washington will actually pay the costs?

**Pape:** Suicide terrorism is strategic coercion, not mainly irrational fanaticism. Pape argues that suicide campaigns usually aim to compel democratic states to withdraw military forces from territory the attackers view as their homeland. The attacker's death functions as a costly signal: the organization tries to show that it can keep imposing civilian pain until the target changes policy.

**Ashworth et al.:** The Pape critique is methodological. They argue that studying only suicide attacks cannot show what causes groups to choose suicide terrorism, because the comparison set of non-suicide resistance is missing. Their warning is the social-science version of a theory trap: even a strong pattern among observed cases does not identify the risk of the outcome unless we know what happens in the broader population.

**Pape reply:** Pape answers that his work studied the universe of suicide terrorism and, in later work, compared occupations that did and did not produce suicide campaigns. His strongest defensible claim is narrower than "occupation causes suicide terrorism" in a simple way: foreign military occupation, especially by a democracy and across religious difference, is a central condition under which suicide terrorism becomes strategically attractive.

## Concepts To Bring To Class

- **Credibility:** A threat matters only if the target believes the sender is willing and able to carry it out. Nuclear weapons and suicide attacks both try to make resolve believable by tying the actor to high-cost escalation.
- **Costly signaling:** Suicide attacks and nuclear brinkmanship are extreme versions of the same logic from earlier weeks. The signal works because it is hard to fake.
- **Coercion vs. deterrence:** Nuclear threats often try to prevent action; suicide terrorism often tries to compel withdrawal. Both ask whether punishment can change policy.
- **Selection problem:** Pape's data show what suicide terrorism cases look like. Ashworth et al. ask what we can infer without comparing them to cases where groups did not use suicide tactics.
- **Democratic vulnerability:** Pape treats democracies as especially susceptible because civilian casualties generate political pressure. That claim should be tested, not assumed.

## One Clean Synthesis

The shared mechanism is **strategic credibility under high-cost violence**. North Korea's nuclear posture, U.S. extended deterrence, and suicide terrorism all operate in a bargaining environment where cheap talk fails. Actors therefore use dangerous signals to convince opponents that continued resistance will be more costly than concession. The difference is the audience and moral economy: nuclear coercion threatens catastrophic escalation between states, while suicide terrorism uses civilian punishment by nonstate actors to coerce democracies. The methodological debate matters because policy prescriptions depend on whether the signal is merely associated with certain conditions or actually caused by them.

## Good Discussion Questions

1. If suicide terrorism and nuclear brinkmanship are both costly signals, what makes one seem "strategic" and the other seem "irrational" in public debate?
2. Does Pape need to prove occupation is a sufficient cause of suicide terrorism, or is it enough to show that occupation is a recurring necessary condition?
3. In the North Korea case, does nuclear capability increase Pyongyang's bargaining power because use is likely, or because even a small probability of use changes U.S. expected costs?
